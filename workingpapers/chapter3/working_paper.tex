\begin{abstract}
Did domestic money creation drive the inflation of Chile's Unidad Popular
(1970--1973), or accommodate external bottlenecks and distributive conflict?
This paper argues that the transmission of domestic liquidity was governed by
the central bank's command over foreign exchange. Grounding dependency theory
in open-economy balance sheets, I formalize the Central Bank of Chile's balance
sheet as an asymmetric structure: domestic currency liabilities it could issue
against international reserve assets it could not. Using monthly central-bank
and IMF series for 1960--1980, I estimate Granger-precedence tests and a
Threshold Vector Autoregression conditioned on the reserve-coverage growth gap.
Across the sample, reverse accommodation dominates forward transmission: price
shocks predict subsequent base-money growth four times more strongly than base
money predicts inflation. The external reserve buffer governs international
pass-through: world price shocks exert no statistically significant effect on
domestic inflation when reserve backing is maintained, but generate 6.5
percentage points of cumulative inflation once money outgrows reserves. The
evidence indicates that domestic monetary expansion operated primarily as an
endogenous accommodation valve under acute balance-of-payments distress.
\end{abstract}
