### H1 — ARDL / PSS Bounds-Test Inference Language

- **Rationale:** The Pesaran, Shin, and Smith (2001) bounds test evaluates the null hypothesis of no long-run levels relationship between variables of mixed integration orders ($I(0)$ and $I(1)$). It is not itself a residual stationarity or residual unit-root test. Treating rejection as proof of stationary residuals overstates the direct output of the bounds procedure.
- **Location 1:** `sections/04_econometric_replication.tex` (Subsection 4.1, lines 53–54)
  - **Before:**
    ```latex
    Under this hypothesis, $\pi_y$ and $\pi_k$ are the coefficients on the lagged levels of output and capital. If the null is true, the regression is spurious because the residuals remain nonstationary ($I(1)$), rendering any derived capacity path econometrically invalid. To evaluate the Wald statistic, I use the critical bounds generated by \citet{Pesaran2001}. These bounds establish two thresholds: a lower bound assuming all variables are $I(0)$, and an upper bound assuming all variables are $I(1)$. The null of no cointegration is rejected only when the computed $F$-statistic exceeds the upper bound, confirming that the output--capital relation yields stationary residuals.
    ```
  - **After:**
    ```latex

If the statistic does not exceed the relevant upper bound, the specification does not provide sufficient evidence of a long-run levels relationship and therefore cannot support the residual-based capacity construction as an identified long-run equilibrium. To evaluate the Wald statistic, I use the critical bounds generated by \citet{Pesaran2001}. ### H4 — Profit-Rate Decomposition Logic

- **Rationale:** In the classical profit-rate decomposition $r_t = \pi_t \mu_t (Y^p_t / K_t)$, sub-unitary transformation elasticity ($\theta < 1$) exerts downward pressure on potential capacity relative to capital ($Y^p_t / K_t$) and hence on potential profitability. An increase in the profit share $\pi_t$ can partly offset this in the *realized* profit rate $r_t$. However, operating below normal capacity ($\mu_t < 1$) further *depresses* realized profitability—it cannot "counteract" declining potential profitability.
- **Location:** `sections/05_discussion_conclusion.tex` (Discussion, line 12)
  - **Before:**
    ```latex
    When $\theta < 1.0$, however, the corporate sector operates under conditions of structural overaccumulation: capital equipment accumulates faster than potential output expands. This creates a persistent downward drag on the maximum profit rate that can only be counteracted by intensifying exploitation (raising $\pi_t$) or tolerating chronic excess capacity.
    ```
  - **After:**
    ```latex
    When $\theta < 1.0$, however, the corporate sector operates under conditions of structural overaccumulation: capital equipment accumulates faster than potential output expands, placing downward pressure on $Y^p_t / K_t$ and, conditional on distribution, on potential profitability. A higher profit share ($\pi_t$) can partly offset this pressure in the realized profit rate, whereas utilization below normal capacity ($\mu_t < 1$) further depresses realized profitability.
    ```
