\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\textbf{1.1} In the history of economic thought, progress rarely unfolds as a linear, cumulative ascent toward universal truth \citep{Roncaglia2006}. As Alessandro Roncaglia demonstrated in his epistemological defense of the discipline, theoretical advance is governed by a \textit{competitive view}: an enduring contest among rival paradigms rooted in fundamentally distinct conceptualizations, analytical categories, and visions of the world. For over half a century, the historiography of Chile's Unidad Popular (UP, 1970--1973) under Salvador Allende has been interpreted through an uncritical cumulative lens. Mainstream accounts project neoclassical factor-clearing assumptions and closed-system monetary axioms onto peripheral reality, treating the Chilean breakdown as a settled cautionary tale of populist fiscal profligacy. Yet beneath this orthodox consensus lies an unexamined failure of conceptualization: treating the peripheral state as a closed monetary system, blind to the structural boundaries imposed by the world market, international currency hierarchies, and balance-of-payments constraints \citep{Vasudevan2009,Basu2022}.

\textbf{1.2} This chapter investigates how central bank reserve solvency mediates the inflationary transmission of domestic money creation during peripheral socialist transformation. If money creation mechanically drove inflation, why did base money expand by over 100\% in 1971 while monthly inflation remained quiescent at 1.5--2.0\% and annual inflation fell from 35\% to 22\%? The answer lies in balance-sheet solvency: in 1971, the Central Bank possessed \$400M in inherited foreign reserves and industry operated with ample idle capacity ($\mu \approx 82\%$), absorbing liquidity through real transactions demand. Inflation pass-through did not exist in 1971---it emerged only in mid-1972 once reserves fell below \$30M and the international credit blockade severed revolving commercial trade lines. Broad money growth functioned as an endogenous accommodation valve through which the central bank mediated an imperial balance-of-payments blockade and intense distributive conflict, contesting the monetarist thesis of \citet{Edwards2024} and the Austrian interpretation of \citet{EspinosaVejar2025}. I formalize the central bank balance sheet through the Solvency Ratio---the ratio of convertible foreign reserve assets to domestic monetary liabilities ($\text{SolvR}_t \equiv \frac{e_t \cdot IR_t}{M_{1,t}}$)---identifying it as the state-dependent threshold governing monetary transmission. My formal threshold tests decisively reject linear and two-regime models in favor of a three-regime architecture extending the Financial Instability Hypothesis of \citet{Minsky1986} to open-economy central banking under international currency hierarchies \citep{Foley2003,ChamorroFutinico2025}. In the solvent Hedge regime ($\text{SolvR}_t > 0.21$), foreign reserve backing absorbs domestic liquidity, holding monetary pass-through to prices at zero. In the intermediate Speculative regime ($0.14 < \text{SolvR}_t \le 0.21$), reserve erosion strains credibility and generates moderate price pass-through ($+0.54\%$). When external credit embargoes and private capital flight breach the lower threshold ($\text{SolvR}_t \le 0.14$), the central bank enters a Forced Ponzi regime, where defensive liquidity accommodation to maintain productive solvency passes directly into hyperinflation ($+4.54\%$) and currency flight.

\textbf{1.3} Allende's thousand days unfolded within the tectonic shifts of the global structural crisis of capitalism of the early 1970s. The unilateral suspension of dollar-gold convertibility by the United States on August 15, 1971 dismantled the Bretton Woods system, detonating world commodity stagflation and intensifying imperial conflict over peripheral natural resources \citep{Harmer2011,Arboleda2023}. Simultaneously, the secular post-war decline of the corporate profit rate across the advanced capitalist core \citep{Weisskopf1979,BowlesGordonWeisskopf1983} narrowed the geopolitical room for peripheral developmentalism. The collapse of the Chilean socialist transition represented the structural collision of domestic redistributive aspirations with external financial subordination under an unstable international monetary order.

\textbf{1.4} Mainstream and neoclassical syntheses characterize the Allende administration through the paradigm of ``macroeconomic populism'' \citep{DornbuschEdwards1990,LarranMeller1991}. In this interpretation, redistributive governments inevitably trace an unforced four-phase cycle: initial reactivation through idle capacity, the emergence of bottleneck shortages, unsustainable fiscal and monetary financing, and final economic collapse. By treating the central bank as an unconstrained printing press and monetary emission as an unmediated political choice, this literature commits two deep epistemological errors. First, it succumbs to what \citet{BlancoMatute2018} formulate as the \emph{illusion of causality}---a cognitive heuristic that infers direct causal generation from the mere temporal coincidence of salient events (the 1970 election, 1971 wage readjustments, and 1972--1973 inflation) while ignoring non-contingent external covariates. Second, it exercises what \citet{IschEtAl2026} classify as \emph{narrative license}---the rhetorical escalation of observational and correlational snapshots into ironclad causal laws to satisfy academic demands for compelling monocausal parables. By analyzing annual aggregates in an institutional vacuum, mainstream models mistake the physical symptoms of organized distribution sabotage and external financial asphyxiation for the mechanical results of domestic monetary policy.

\textbf{1.5} To discriminate between these contested claims on rigorous empirical ground, I deploy an applied Lakatosian evaluation of contested scientific research programmes (MSRP) in economic thought \citep{Lakatos1978,Roncaglia2006,KingSznajder2006,Kvangraven2021}. This methodological framework appraises competing programmes by distinguishing their immutable theoretical axioms (\emph{hard core}) from the auxiliary specifications of their empirical models (\emph{protective belt}), evaluating whether empirical anomalies are absorbed through theoretically progressive extensions that predict \emph{novel facts} or through ad hoc degenerating immunizing stratagems:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
	\item \textbf{The Monetarist Programme ($H_1$, \citealp{Edwards2024}):} Its hard core asserts exogenous base money creation, fractional-reserve multiplier causality ($H \to M1 \to P$), and long-run monetary neutrality in a closed system. Faced with the empirical anomaly of non-inflationary 100\% monetary growth in 1971, its protective belt degenerates into ad hoc narrative stratagems that treat the \$294.0M external financing collapse, the U.S. Eximbank blockade, and domestic distribution sabotage as incidental background noise.
	\item \textbf{The Austrian Business-Cycle Programme ($H_2$, \citealp{EspinosaVejar2025}):} Its hard core posits that artificially suppressed interest rates and credit expansion distort intertemporal price signals, inducing a malinvestment boom in higher-order, roundabout capital goods ($\Delta K \gg 0$), whose inevitable coordination failure precipitates systemic breakdown.
	\item \textbf{The Structural-Dependency Programme ($H_3$, \citealp{Foley2003,Vasudevan2009,ChamorroFutinico2025}):} Advanced in this chapter, its hard core roots peripheral macroeconomics in international currency hierarchies, balance-of-payments dominance, conflict inflation, and credit money endogeneity ($\text{Loans} \to \text{Deposits} \to \text{Reserves}$). Its protective belt extends Minsky's financial fragility taxonomy to the central bank balance sheet ($\text{SolvR}_t \equiv e_t \cdot IR_t / M_{1,t}$), achieving a progressive problemshift that successfully predicts the novel empirical fact of state-dependent threshold non-linearity: domestic liquidity expansion generates zero consumer price inflation under solvent reserve buffers ($\text{SolvR}_t > 0.21$), jumping into explosive pass-through only when external asphyxiation breaches the Forced Ponzi threshold ($\text{SolvR}_t \le 0.14$).
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{1.6} This chapter deepens the theoretical foundations established in Chapter~2. Whereas Chapter~2 identified the external boundary of accumulation through an annual balance-of-payments threshold ($\gamma = 0.425$) that throttles mechanization under unbalanced growth, this chapter uncovers the dynamic balance-sheet mechanism through which that external boundary operates. To avoid temporal aggregation bias, I coordinate two empirical tiers: a secular annual panel (1920--2010, $T=91$) and a high-frequency monthly archival panel (1960--1980, $N=250$ continuous months). At the annual frequency, $H_2$ fails decisively: gross fixed investment in machinery and equipment contracted ($-2.3\%$) during the 1971 expansion while structural capacity utilization surged to an all-time peak of $\mu_{1971} = 0.9844$, confirming Keynesian-Kaleckian reserve reactivation rather than Austrian capital lengthening. At the monthly frequency, the 3-regime TVAR and event studies capture the opening of a relational trade account scissors operating across two distinct domains. On the physical and real purchasing power domain, Prebisch-ECLAC capacity to import ($M^{\text{cap}}_t$) collapsed to $27.7$ following the August 1971 Nixon Shock and copper price decline, blowing open the Real Structural Imbalance ($\Theta_t \equiv M_t / M^{\text{cap}}_t \times 100$, operationalizing the Latin American structuralist concept of \textit{desequilibrio estructural externo}) to an unsustainable peak of $283.4$ in May 1973. This real physical imbalance constitutes the certified structural shock driving industrial bottlenecks and inflation in our DAG causal discovery. Concurrently, on the nominal and exchange-rate domain, the Relative Price Disparity Index ($\Omega_t \approx 1/\text{RER}_t$) exploded to $190.6$ under the official parity peg ($e_t = 0.0122$). Because imperial credit embargoes enforced hard quantity rationing on physical imports in foreign exchange, $\Omega_t$ diverged from effective import volumes, recording the nominal price disparity preserved by the state's defense of the social wage. 

\textbf{1.7} The remainder of this chapter is structured as follows. Section~\ref{sec:literature_review} surveys the contested traditions in Chilean political economy and formalizes the epistemological critique of orthodox causality. Section~\ref{sec:macro_framework} develops the theoretical framework of World Money, monetary endogeneity, and the open-economy transposition of Minskyan solvency regimes to the peripheral central bank. Section~\ref{sec:empirical_tournament} grounds the inquiry in the historical political economy of the pre-1970 accumulation regime, details the multi-frequency data architecture, specifies the model restrictions, and implements the econometric evaluation across the four-stage empirical strategy. Section~\ref{sec:discussion_conclusion} discusses the political economy of peripheral solvency, situates the UP within the global structural crisis of the 1970s, and outlines lessons for contemporary monetary sovereignty.

\section{Contested Approaches to the Political Economy of the Rise and Fall of the Unidad Popular}
\label{sec:literature_review}

\textbf{2.1} No single framework commands consensus on the political economy of the rise and fall of Chile's Unidad Popular (UP). Rival explanatory traditions have organized the field instead, and they emerged under sharply unequal historical conditions. Transnational networks linking the Pontificia Universidad Católica de Chile to the University of Chicago consolidated the neoliberal school, which then acquired state power through the dictatorship that followed the 1973 coup. Neo-structuralism emerged under that same dictatorship as a technocratic opposition and later became the preferred economic language of the post-dictatorship order. Historical Political Economy entered much later, bringing powerful tools of causal identification to historical questions, but entering a disciplinary environment already shaped by earlier victories and exclusions. Dependency theory occupies a different position altogether. It was the intellectual world from which the UP programme itself emerged, and the coup shattered it institutionally before it could resolve the theoretical problems it had opened.

\textbf{2.2} These traditions did not simply coexist. They succeeded one another through conflict. Each identified part of the problem, and none integrated external transmission, distributive conflict, and macroeconomic dynamics into a single explanatory sequence. This section critically evaluates these traditions. It reconstructs the historical conditions under which each approach became authoritative, clarifies the mechanisms each privileged, and shows which questions each rendered visible and which it displaced, sanitized, or foreclosed. It begins with classical dependency theory, which posed the problem the chapter as a whole seeks to resolve more sharply than any approach that followed.

\subsection{Classical Dependency Theory: Interrupted Programme}
\label{sec:A1_classical_deptheory}

\textbf{2.3} Dependency theory did not approach the Unidad Popular from the outside. It helped constitute the intellectual horizon within which the UP became thinkable. Its formation in Chile brought together two lineages that converged, institutionally and theoretically, in Santiago during the long sixties. From one side came the Monthly Review tradition, beginning with \citet{Baran1957}'s analysis of economic surplus under monopoly capitalism and radicalized by \citet{Frank1967} into the development-of-underdevelopment thesis: underdevelopment was not a residual stage but an active product of incorporation into the world capitalist system. From the other came the structuralist school built within the headquarters of the Economic Commission for Latin America (ECLAC), led by Raúl Prebisch, which had already framed Latin American development through centre--periphery relations, external bottlenecks, and the historical asymmetries of international trade.

\textbf{2.4} At the Centro de Estudios Socioeconómicos (CESO) of the Universidad de Chile, the two lineages fused into a common research programme. Theotônio dos Santos, Vania Bambirra, André Gunder Frank, Orlando Caputo, and Roberto Pizarro, in close dialogue with Ruy Mauro Marini in Concepción, transformed dependency from a general critique of peripheral capitalism into an attempt to specify the historical forms linking regional subordination, accumulation, and political strategy in Latin America \citep{DosSantos1970,Bambirra1971,CaputoPizarro1970,Kay2010,CardenasLanaSeabra2022}.

\textbf{2.5} The programme's analytical wager was clear. Peripheral capitalism followed a distinct developmental dynamic structured by external constraints, a bourgeoisie incapable of autonomous development, and patterns of accumulation that continually reproduced subordination. Within this tradition, socialist transformation was an objective developmental necessity grounded in the structural limits of peripheral capital accumulation. Yet the tradition never achieved theoretical unity. Marini's account of superexploitation pushed the analysis toward a world-market logic in which the systematic compression of labour conditioned domestic reproduction; Cardoso and Faletto insisted instead that dependency took historically specific forms depending on the configuration of domestic class alliances and the relative autonomy of the state \citep{Marini1973,CardosoFaletto1979}. The tension was decisive. At issue was whether domestic political mediation constituted an independent object of analysis or followed largely from world-market imperatives. The tradition posed the question with great force and never settled it.

\textbf{2.6} That unresolved tension did not prevent dependency theory from producing the richest contemporaneous political-economy account of the UP. \citet{DeVylder1976}'s \textit{Allende's Chile} remains a seminal account seldom referenced in Chilean debates: a participant-observer reconstruction of the UP as a process shaped by the interaction of internal class dynamics and external constraint. Around it emerged a broad empirical corpus tracking the same intertwined problem from complementary angles---distributional conflict across successive governments, worker and peasant radicalization from below, the external credit squeeze and spare-parts embargo, the monetary and balance-of-payments mechanisms through which external pressure became domestic crisis, and the unresolved strategic problem of the state under conditions of dual power \citep{StallingsZimbalist1975,Stallings1978,EspinosaZimbalist1978,Kay1978,PetrasMorley1975,GriffithJones1981,Sideri1979,SweezyMagdoff1974,Zavaleta1974,Winn1986}. The tradition's strength lay precisely here. It did not treat external intervention and domestic conflict as competing explanations; it approached them as interacting dimensions of the same historical process.

\textbf{2.7} That achievement also marked the limit dependency theory could not overcome in its classical form. The tradition posed the integrated problem correctly: how external subordination, domestic class struggle, and macroeconomic instability became mutually conditioning dynamics in peripheral capitalism. It lacked a unified theory of the state and a formal specification of the transmission mechanisms linking external shocks to domestic distributive and macroeconomic outcomes. Its categories were historically powerful, but the internal architecture required to operationalize them remained unfinished. The weakness lay in the modelling rather than the diagnosis. The tradition identified the decisive mechanisms and could not yet represent them in an integrated way.

\textbf{2.8} The defeat of dependency theory was twofold. Politically, the coup dismantled the institutions, networks, and research centres in which the programme had been elaborated. Intellectually, the interruption arrived before the tradition could work through its own internal tensions. No superior explanatory framework refuted it. It broke under conditions in which theoretical incompletion coincided with the collapse of Chile's liberal democracy, the failure of democratic socialism to resolve the crisis under organized domestic and international sabotage, and the installation of a subsequent civic-military dictatorship. Subsequent approaches entered a field already reorganized by force, not reason. The next school to become authoritative in Chilean political economy was not the one that best resolved the problem dependency had identified, but the one that arrived coordinated with the counterrevolutionary reconstruction of the state.

\subsection{The Rise of the Chicago Boys and the Neo-liberal School}
\label{sec:A2_neoliberal_chicago}

\textbf{2.9} The neoliberal school did not emerge from within the intellectual world of the Unidad Popular as one interpretation among others. It entered by a different route: as a transnationally trained programme of economic reconstruction that acquired authority through the counterrevolutionary reorganization of the Chilean state. Its genealogy is now well established. The 1956 agreement between the Pontificia Universidad Católica de Chile and the University of Chicago opened the institutional channel through which Milton Friedman, Arnold Harberger, and Gary Becker trained a cohort of Chilean economists in monetarism and public finance orthodoxy. By the late 1960s that network had outgrown its academic form. It had become a politically connected project, tied to the Chilean right and increasingly oriented toward a systemic restructuring of Chilean capitalism \citep{Fischer2009,Valdes1995,Clark2017}.

\textbf{2.10} That programme found its canonical expression in \textit{El Ladrillo}\footnote{\textit{``The Brick''} in English.}. The document matters because it codified in advance the economic architecture on which the post-UP order would be constructed. It operated as a \emph{counterrevolutionary vanguard design}: a strategic programme prepared before the coup to direct the reconstruction of Chilean capitalism once the popular government was overthrown.

\textbf{2.11} Its diagnostic identified a familiar set of problems: persistent inflation, external disequilibria, agricultural stagnation, low growth, and distortions associated with state-led development. Some of these observations captured real structural tensions within Chilean capitalism, and they deserve acknowledgement on their own terms. The argument that prolonged relative-price distortions hindered export diversification and contributed to balance-of-payments stress cannot simply be dismissed, nor can the claim that developmentalist policy generated enduring macroeconomic tensions \citep{DeCastro1992}. The broader theoretical hypothesis holding these observations together was much weaker: that the crisis of the UP merely culminated a long secular drift toward the socialization of the economy. That claim recoded the conjuncture normatively to advance a project of market discipline. It did not explain it.

\textbf{2.12} Here lay the neoliberal school's constitutive limit. It could register imbalances, but only by stripping them of their political-economy content. Distribution appeared not as class conflict but as a problem of distorted price signals. External crisis became the endogenous consequence of statist excess, never the interaction between domestic struggle and international destabilization. The October 1972 lockout, the U.S. credit squeeze, the spare-parts embargo, and the broader campaign of destabilization could not enter the account without undermining the framework's own premises. Neoliberalism did not omit these elements by accident. Its explanatory architecture required them to disappear, since admitting them would have meant abandoning the very theory that made the crisis legible as proof of the impossibility of socialist transformation.

\textbf{2.13} The neoliberal interpretation thus became authoritative on conditions that were not primarily intellectual. Its rise reflected no superior integration of external transmission, distributive conflict, and macroeconomic dynamics. A programme prepared before the coup entered a field reorganized by force, and the dictatorship then installed it as the common sense of economic reconstruction. That reconstruction dismantled the institutions of political mediation and collective decision-making while relying, paradoxically, on the planning capacities of the developmental state it claimed to supersede \citep{Clark2017,Bockman2019}. The neoliberal school was less a successful solution to the explanatory problem dependency had posed in the periphery than a victorious political project presenting itself as economic science. Its enduring relevance lies precisely there: it turned one historically specific resolution of crisis into a universal lesson about the alleged impossibility of any alternative to market coordination \citep{Espinosa2021}.

\subsection{The Neo-Structuralist School: Selective Sanitization}
\label{sec:A3_neo_structuralism}

\textbf{2.14} The neo-structuralist school was an intellectual accomplishment of the post-dictatorship order, not a simple continuation of classical structuralism. It built its institutional base under dictatorship, above all in the CIEPLAN research centres, where former structuralists and opposition economists monitored the Chicago Boys' experiment while preparing an alternative programme for democratic transition. That genealogy explains much of the school's subsequent authority. The CIEPLAN economists were more than moderates who had softened their earlier commitments under adverse conditions \citep{Silva1991}. They became the technocratic fraction through which the post-dictatorship centre-left could criticize orthodox neoliberalism while remaining within a framework compatible with profit-oriented, market-disciplined peripheral capitalist development. Neo-structuralism entered the field as a politically serviceable renovation suited to Chile's integration into globalized modernization \citep{Sklair2000,Leiva2008,Maillet2016}.

\textbf{2.15} The decisive theoretical operation of that renovation was the abandonment of the surplus approach. Where Prebisch and the earlier ECLAC tradition had centered the appropriation and reinvestment of surplus under peripheral conditions, neo-structuralism recast the problem in the language of market failure, macroprudence, and pragmatic state intervention. The shift did not simply update structuralism. It altered its theoretical object. The external constraint remained, but increasingly as a macroeconomic restriction rather than as a political economy of surplus appropriation and dependent reproduction. Neo-structuralism reformulated a theory of historically structured subordination as a problem of policy calibration. This analytical retreat explains why \citet{Leiva2008}'s diagnosis remains so essential: without the surplus approach, the external constraint survives only in a thinner form, legible as a balance-of-payments constraint but stripped of its class content and of the social relations that reproduce it. Neo-structuralism did not abandon empirical rigour. It abandoned the only framework in which the external constraint and distributive conflict could be theorized together as moments of the same accumulation process.

\textbf{2.16} The school's account of the UP crystallized most clearly around Bitar's contribution. Originally published in 1979 and repeatedly revisited thereafter, \citet{Bitar2013} offered the most sophisticated neo-structuralist reconstruction of the UP's crisis. Its great strength lay in refusing a purely monetary reading of the balance of payments. Bitar sought to integrate external constraint, political opposition, and structural transformation into a single narrative, and his concept of a ``viability area'' remains a genuine analytical contribution: the shrinking space within which the UP programme might have advanced without precipitating macroeconomic collapse. The achievement is important and merits direct recognition. But it also established the school's defining omission. Across multiple editions, Bitar never substantively engaged the dependency literature that had already developed around the same empirical terrain. Accounts from the classical dependency tradition such as \citet{DeVylder1976} and \citet{Stallings1978} were available, and Bitar never confronted them. The silence was no bibliographic accident. It marked the point at which the school displaced dependency's central problem from legitimate post-dictatorship economic reasoning.

\textbf{2.17} That displacement became hegemonic through the populist-macro paradigm. \citet{LarranMeller1991} absorbed the UP into a four-phase sequence of initial success, emerging disequilibrium, macroeconomic deterioration, and collapse, while \citet{DornbuschEdwards1991} generalized this logic into a regional diagnosis of ``economic populism.'' The construction had real analytical power. It offered a clean narrative, identifiable turning points, and policy lessons that travelled throughout the region in the aftermath of dictatorship. But that was also its function. By defining redistributive macroeconomic strategy as ``macroeconomic populism,'' the paradigm foreclosed the possibility that wage-led expansion might, under some conditions, be analytically sustainable rather than inherently self-defeating. \citet{Meller2000} consolidated the mainstream narrative of the UP: macroeconomic irresponsibility combined with political polarization. The result was a reading self-critical in tone but exculpatory in effect, in which the programme failed because of its own excesses, not because of the interaction among external constraint, class conflict, and institutional sabotage. Sergio \citet{Ramos1979} and Joseph \citet{Ramos1980} capture the contrast with unusual clarity: the former treated inflation as a contradiction internal to a process of structural transformation under class struggle, whereas the latter recast crisis as a problem of disequilibrium management and stabilization failure.

\textbf{2.18} Neo-structuralism actively replaced the conflict-centered structuralist problematique available from within the UP's own intellectual production with a depoliticized substitute \citep{LarranMeller1991,DornbuschEdwards1991,Meller2000}. The school's conceptual limitations constituted strategic omissions built directly into the terms of its post-dictatorship authority. Its primary accomplishment was to preserve elements of the structuralist external-constraint framework while converting them into the language of macroeconomic governance that would sustain two decades of centre-left hegemony. In doing so, it recast the thousand days of Allende's government as a cautionary tale about redistributive excess. The school functioned as the progressive idiom through which post-dictatorship neoliberal hegemony could be criticized, managed, and stabilized without reopening the class-conflict categories that had made earlier structuralist and dependency traditions politically dangerous for the ruling classes \citep{Leiva2008}.

\textbf{2.19} This assessment does not deny the analytical substance of authors such as Bitar or Meller. It specifies the price of their renovation. The inclusion of monetary, structural, and political variables in an integrated narrative was a real gain. The exclusion of class conflict as an endogenous economic force was the condition under which that gain became institutionally admissible. That is why neo-structuralism remains relevant beyond its own immediate milieu. It became the default macroeconomic memory inherited by the next generation of scholarship, including the field that would later arrive with stronger empirical tools but without a comparable theory of the underlying mechanism.

\subsection{Historical Political Economy: Methods Under Inherited Macro Theory}
\label{sec:A4_historical_PE}

\textbf{2.20} Historical Political Economy (HPE) represents a genuine methodological advance in the study of economic history. By bringing the tools of the credibility revolution into historical inquiry, it has generated a level of empirical precision that earlier traditions rarely achieved. Its institutional consolidation around the \textit{Journal of Historical Political Economy} and the \textit{Oxford Handbook of Historical Political Economy} reflects a broader ambition to recover historical questions for economists without sacrificing standards of causal identification \citep{JenkinsRubin2024,CharnyshFinkelGehlbach2023}. In the Chilean case, that ambition has yielded an impressive body of work on prices, wages, inequality, agrarian reform, and political violence. No serious assessment of the field can dismiss these achievements.

\textbf{2.21} HPE did not create the empirical terrain on which it operates in Chile. That terrain also rests on a broader renewal of Chilean economic history, which has reconstructed long-run series on prices, trade, living standards, and distribution with considerable archival depth. The work of Mario Matus, Manuel Llorca-Jaña, Claudio Robles, and related scholars belongs to that growing economic-history field, which has made more ambitious forms of historical political economy empirically possible, rather than to HPE in the stricter disciplinary sense. Their contribution is foundational: they help constitute the quantitative and historical terrain on which later identification-driven work has gained traction. \citet{RodriguezWeber2018} matters especially for this chapter and dissertation, because the infrastructure built for long-run inequality series also underpins the wage-share series employed here, assembled through the work of \citet{Astorga2023}. That long-run account places the rise and fall of Allende's government within a broader cycle of declining inequality and explicitly recognizes coercive distribution as a determinant of income shares, providing a hinge between economic history and the larger body of political-economy questions at stake.

\textbf{2.22} HPE proper enters at a different level, combining historical questions with explicit identification strategies. \citet{AldunateGonzalezPrem2022} isolate the firm-level effects of U.S. banking covert action during the Allende years, while \citet{JaimovichToledo2021} trace the long-run persistence of Mapuche territorial conflict to the uneven geography of agrarian reform and counter-reform. \citet{GirardiBowles2018} should be read somewhat differently. Their event-study of the Santiago stock exchange provides a micro-level account of financial-market volatility in defense of private property, and for that reason forms a useful hinge toward dependency theory's concern with financial subordination. These are major contributions, but they do not all do the same work. Some build the empirical archive, others identify causal effects, and a few open questions that point beyond HPE's inherited macroeconomic frame.

\textbf{2.23} That limitation appears most clearly in the work of Felipe González and his collaborators, who stand at the centre of the contemporary HPE literature on Chile \citep{Gonzalez2013,GonzalezVial2021,AldunateGonzalezPrem2022,GonzalezPremUrzua2020,BautistaGonzalezMartinezMunozPrem2023,CuestaGallegoGonzalez2015,GonzalezPrem2026}. Their contributions are methodologically exemplary: they have built new archival datasets, deployed transparent causal-identification strategies, and opened major empirical questions on agrarian reform, nationalization, covert U.S. intervention, and the institutional legacies of dictatorship. González's research programme represents the field at its strongest. Yet it also reveals the field's defining limit. The economy appears primarily as a terrain on which political and institutional effects can be measured, not as the constitutive terrain of class conflict, accumulation, and crisis. Even where the underlying materials point toward broader macroeconomic questions, the explanatory frame typically comes from post-dictatorship mainstream accounts rather than from within historical political economy itself. The result is a body of scholarship of very high empirical quality that displaces political economy in the classical sense into a micro-political science of institutions, interventions, and outcomes.

\textbf{2.24} This boundary stems directly from the field's disciplinary constitution. HPE approached the Chilean case after the earlier conflict among explanatory traditions had already been settled institutionally, inheriting a macroeconomic vocabulary shaped by the post-dictatorship synthesis that recoded the UP as a problem of macroeconomic populism and balance-of-payments disequilibrium. The framework developed here builds upon HPE's empirical advances while embedding them within an explanatory programme capable of treating external transmission, distributive conflict, and macroeconomic dynamics as mutually conditioning structural processes.

\subsection{The Revival of Dependency Theory as a \textit{Research Programme}}
\label{sec:A5_rev_deptheory}

\textbf{2.25} The return to dependency theory recovers a vital research programme whose historical retreat coincided with a broader contraction of developmental horizons across Latin America. As \citet{Palma2016} suggests, the demise of dependency theory and structuralism altered academic debate while impoverishing the region's capacity to conceive development as a problem of autonomy, structural transformation, and historical agency. The analytical imperative today is to reconstruct dependency as a progressive framework capable of generating novel, falsifiable empirical explanations while reclaiming the systemic alternatives that orthodox models foreclose.

\textbf{2.26} That is the significance of \citet{Kvangraven2021}'s intervention. Recasting dependency theory through Lakatos's notion of a scientific research programme, she argues that its hard core remains analytically productive: global capitalism tends to reproduce hierarchical relations between core and periphery, and those relations condition the developmental corridor within which peripheral accumulation unfolds. The move is more than an epistemological rescue operation. It is a methodological directive. It restores dependency theory as a body of scholarship capable of disagreement in auxiliary hypotheses while retaining a common research orientation. The revival asks whether the tradition can once again generate novel, empirically verified explanations of peripheral reproduction.

\textbf{2.27} The programme's contemporary reconstruction becomes sharper in \citet{MadariagaPalestini2021}. Their key distinction is between \textit{situations of dependency} and \textit{mechanisms of dependency}: between the historically concrete manifestations of peripheral capitalism and the causal processes that reproduce external conditioning. The distinction is decisive for the present chapter. What the classical tradition identified with great force but could not fully formalize was precisely the transmission between external constraint and internal dynamics. The renewed programme begins to close that gap, not by abandoning historical contingency, but by specifying the mechanisms through which external hierarchies shape domestic accumulation, class structure, and political conflict. Around this reformulation, dependency theory has again become a toolkit for the comparative-historical analysis of peripheral capitalisms, rent, and externally conditioned development \citep{BurchardtDietzWarnecke2021,Bizberg2021}.

\textbf{2.28} The revival extends beyond epistemological rehabilitation. It has taken substantive form in one of dependency theory's original sites of elaboration: Santiago, Chile. That matters both symbolically and analytically. What makes the contemporary Santiago-based scholarship important is not simply that it returns to dependency in the abstract, but that it develops new empirical evidence and new diagnoses from within the tradition's own problematic. \citet{AhumadaTorres2024} reconstruct dependency theory as a still-productive framework for understanding asymmetries between central and peripheral capitalisms, while \citet{TorresAhumada2022} restate the centre--periphery vision as a general lens for interpreting contemporary global capitalism and its rentier tendencies.

\textbf{2.29} The same agenda then moves into concrete institutional and macro-structural analysis. \citet{Ruiz2021} analyzes the Chilean pension system as an institutional mechanism of dependent financialization. \citet{AhumadaMendezSossdorfJara2025} identify a structural disconnection between surplus capture and productive investment in contemporary Chile, showing how rent extraction can persist without productive upgrading. From the sphere of external insertion, \citet{Sossdorf2024Trade} shows that Chile's liberal export strategy has generated a stagnant cycle anchored in static comparative advantages, weak technological dynamism, and limited diversification. Taken together, these works operationalize dependency theory to generate historically grounded explanations of financial subordination, rentier accumulation, and externally constrained development in present-day Chile. Dependency theory is being revived in its place of birth as a dense research programme: historically self-conscious, theoretically explicit, and empirically generative.

\textbf{2.30} The revival also carries a distinct normative horizon, defining development as emancipation from structural subordination. In \citet{Ahumada2023}, this appears as a conception centered on non-dependence: for peripheral formations, genuine development requires building the material basis for collective self-determination beyond mere increases in aggregate productivity or income. \citet{Arboleda2023} sharpens this perspective by recovering the memory of the Unidad Popular as the historical conjuncture in which dependency theory, structural planning, and national liberation converged in Chile. Within that horizon, development signified gaining sovereign control over productive assets from oligarchic and transnational capital, redirecting the surplus toward popular needs, worker self-management, and plural property forms.

\textbf{2.31} That normative content belongs to the framework itself rather than arriving as an external moral add-on. It is part of what made dependency theory politically consequential in Chile, and part of what still gives force to its revival today. Recent work such as \citet{AhumadaChang2025} extends rather than abandons this inheritance: the call to recover policy space and rethink the international order continues a tradition whose emancipatory horizon was condensed, with particular clarity, in the experience and defeat of the Unidad Popular.

\textbf{2.32} What follows takes up that revived horizon at the level of explanation, showing how external constraint, distributive conflict, and macroeconomic dynamics interact within a peripheral economy.

\subsection{Epistemological Critique: The Illusion of Causality, Narrative License, and Collider Traps}
\label{sec:epistemological_critique}

\textbf{2.33} The persistent divergence among these explanatory traditions cannot be resolved through historiographical narrative alone. As \citet{BlancoMatute2018} establish, human causal reasoning is systematically vulnerable to the \emph{illusion of causality}: a robust cognitive bias wherein observers detect strong causal links between co-occurring events even when the statistical contingency is zero or driven by unobserved common causes. In the economic historiography of the Unidad Popular, orthodox monetarist and Austrian accounts observe a conspicuous historical coincidence---the election of a socialist government in late 1970, expansionary fiscal-wage policy in 1971, and the explosion of inflation in 1972--1973. Relying on intuitive heuristics, they assign exclusive causal responsibility to domestic monetary issuance ($g_{M,t} \to \pi_t$), treating international monetary hierarchy, terms-of-trade shifts, and geopolitical destabilization as irrelevant background noise \citep{Edwards2024,EspinosaVejar2025}.

\textbf{2.34} This causal illusion is reinforced by what \citet{IschEtAl2026} identify as \emph{narrative license} and \emph{overreaching causal claims} in historical social science. Orthodox accounts dress up doctrinal priors as ironclad empirical laws without testing alternative causal DAGs or specifying counterfactual identification paths. For example, \citet{EspinosaVejar2025} assert an Austrian Business Cycle mechanism wherein artificial credit expansion generated an unsustainable capital malinvestment boom. Yet they advance this claim without presenting positive evidence of gross fixed capital deepening ($\Delta K$), ignoring that Chilean fixed machinery investment contracted during 1971 while capacity utilization reached historical peaks. Narrative license allows the theorist to impute unobserved microeconomic mechanisms (capital lengthening) to explain macroeconomic outcomes while dismissing external interventions as quantitatively negligible.

\textbf{2.35} Methodologically, orthodox accounts fall into an \emph{M-bias collider trap} \citep{Pearl2009}. In historical narratives of the UP crisis, intermediate symptoms such as retail shortages, food queues, and black markets ($C$) are conditioned upon as proof of ``repressed inflation'' caused by price controls. In structural causal terms, retail shortages ($C$) are an endogenous collider variable caused jointly by domestic demand stimulus ($U_1$) and private wholesale distribution lockouts, trucking strikes, and hoarding ($U_2$). Conditioning on empty retail shelves induces a spurious association between public policy ($U_1$) and private economic sabotage ($U_2$):
\begin{equation}
U_1 \longrightarrow [C] \longleftarrow U_2 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad U_1 \not\perp\!\!\!\perp U_2 \mid C
\end{equation}
By treating retail shortages as a neutral indicator of excess liquidity, orthodox accounts commit a collider stratification error, falsely attributing the supply paralysis orchestrated by private distributor federations exclusively to public deficit monetization.

\textbf{2.36} To transcend these causal illusions, narrative biases, and collider traps, I adopt the neo-Lakatosian methodology of \citet{KingSznajder2006}. I treat Monetarism ($H_1$), Austrian Business Cycle Theory ($H_2$), and Classical Structuralism / Dependency ($H_3$) as rival scientific research traditions with distinct hard cores, auxiliary assumptions, and falsifiable zero restrictions. I submit the core causal claims of these competing traditions to a formal \textbf{Hypothesis Tournament} evaluated across multi-frequency time series data.

\section{World Money, Financial Subordination, and the Peripheral Central Bank Balance Sheet}
\label{sec:macro_framework}
\label{sec:B_FS_WM}

\subsection{World Money, the Universal Equivalent, and the Monetary Expression of Value}
\label{sec:B1_worldmoney_mev}
\label{sec:B1_historymoney}
\label{sec:B2_worldmoney_mev}

\textbf{3.1} In Marxian political economy, money originates as the commodity form of the universal equivalent: the unique social medium whose function is to express the value of all commodities and serve as the general means of payment \citep[\S3.1]{Basu2022}. Gold historically occupied this role not through inherent natural properties, but through a social process of market validation, acting as the bearer of abstract human labor. 

\textbf{3.2} The historical transit to credit money transforms this institutional form without dissolving its underlying function. As banking systems developed to intermediate commercial credit, central bank liabilities emerged as the legal anchor of domestic circulation \citep[\S3.2.3]{Basu2022}. The commodity anchor disappeared from domestic circulation, but the imperative of social validation remained. Central bank liabilities and bank deposits perform the role that bullion once fulfilled: providing the monetary expression through which private, decentralized labor is validated as social labor. Following the Monetary Expression of Value (MEV) framework \citep{Foley1982,Basu2022}, the value of money links aggregate labor-time to the circulating monetary unit within a national formation.

\textbf{3.3} Once circulation crosses national borders, however, credit money confronts the world market. While Marx presented world money in the physical form of bullion shedding national garbs \citep[p.~51]{Ivanova2013}, a global credit-money system reproduces the universal equivalent through an international currency hierarchy \citep{Vasudevan2009,Ivanova2020}. National currencies are stratified by their degree of international acceptability, liquidity, and convertibility. World money is the historically specific form wherein the liabilities of the dominant hegemonic power operate as the universal means of international payment, reserve asset, and unit of account.

\subsection{The Bretton Woods Architecture and Peripheral Balance-of-Payments Constraints}
\label{sec:B2_BrettonWoods_BOP}
\label{sec:B3_BrettonWoods_BOP}

\textbf{3.4} The post-war Bretton Woods architecture codified this currency hierarchy institutionally. By pegging the U.S. dollar to gold at \$35 per ounce while anchoring other currencies to the dollar, the international monetary order formalized the dollar as the operative form of world money \citep[p.~483]{Vasudevan2009}. This arrangement granted the hegemonic center an ``exorbitant privilege'': the ability to borrow internationally in its own currency, finance external deficits without reserve constraints, and act as banker to the world \citep[pp.~243--244]{Vasudevan2019}.

\textbf{3.5} For peripheral economies, this institutional architecture generated an acute structural asymmetry. Peripheral states cannot settle international accounts in their own domestic currencies; external liabilities and essential imports must be discharged in world money. The peripheral circuit of capital confronts an absolute balance-of-payments constraint \citep{Vasudevan2016,Abeles2013}. Domestic credit expansion is bounded by the capacity to command foreign exchange through export earnings, terms of trade, and access to international credit lines. Reserve accumulation constitutes an externally enforced structural prerequisite of economic reproduction \citep[p.~244]{Vasudevan2019}.

\textbf{3.6} The unilateral closure of the gold window by the United States on August 15, 1971---the Nixon Shock---shattered the fixed-parity anchor of Bretton Woods. While this rupture removed gold convertibility, it preserved dollar centrality, ushering in an era of global floating fiat money and commodity stagflation \citep[p.~486]{Vasudevan2009}. For the nascent socialist project of the Unidad Popular, this systemic shock dismantled the international monetary stability upon which its initial macroeconomic projections rested.

\subsection{The Peripheral Central Bank Balance Sheet: From Reserve Adequacy to Leverage Dynamics}
\label{sec:B3_levr}
\label{sec:B4_fs_levR}
\label{sec:C_levr}

\textbf{3.7} Financial subordination materializes concretely on the balance sheet of the peripheral central bank. Mainstream open-economy models interpret this balance sheet through the lens of ``reserve adequacy'' and precautionary self-insurance, treating international reserves as an inventory buffer optimized against friction costs to insulate the economy from sudden stops \citep{Jeanne2007,CespedesChang2024}. This normative framing assumes that reserve accumulation represents the voluntary, unconstrained choice of a sovereign optimizing agent.

\textbf{3.8} In peripheral capitalism, reserve trajectories are the structural outcome of the balance-of-payments constraint, unequal exchange, and imperial financial diplomacy \citep{Vasudevan2009,Abeles2013}. The two sides of the peripheral central bank's balance sheet reflect fundamentally distinct monetary forms. On the liability side, domestic money represents unanchored credit obligations ($M_{1,t}$ or monetary base $H_t$) that the financial system owes the public, underwritten by state authority \citep[\S3.2.3]{Basu2022}. On the asset side, international reserves ($IR_t$, measured in USD millions) consist of claims on world money that the peripheral state cannot emit.

\textbf{3.9} This balance-sheet structure constitutes an active leverage relation:
\begin{equation}
	\text{LevR}_t \;\equiv\; \frac{M_{1,t}}{e_t \cdot IR_t} \;=\; \text{SolvR}_t^{-1}
	\label{eq:leverage_ratio}
\end{equation}
where $e_t$ is the nominal exchange rate (Escudos per USD) and $\text{SolvR}_t$ is the Central Bank Solvency Ratio. Financial subordination acquires its operative expression through this leverage relation: movements in $\text{SolvR}_t$ govern the structural corridor within which domestic macroeconomic policy can maneuver. The complete formal accounting identities and analytical laws of motion governing this balance-sheet relation are documented in Appendix~\ref{app:bop_levr}.

\subsection{Money Endogeneity vs. Exogeneity and the Peripheral Paradox}
\label{sec:B4_money_endogeneity_paradox}
\label{sec:money_endogeneity_paradox}

\textbf{3.10} Grounding the peripheral balance sheet requires resolving the foundational debate over the causality between bank credit, deposits, and central bank reserves \citep{RochonRossi2013,Lavoie2014}. Mainstream monetarism and orthodox macroeconomics operate under Fractional Reserve Theory (FRT) and loanable-funds axioms: the central bank exogenously fixes the monetary base ($H_t$), which mechanically dictates bank lending and broad money through a stable fractional-reserve multiplier ($H \to \text{Deposits} \to \text{Loans} \to P$), asserting a unitary elasticity in log levels ($\ln M1_t = \alpha + \beta \ln H_t + \varepsilon_t$, with $\beta = 1, \alpha = 0$). In sharp contrast, Post-Keynesian Endogenous Money Theory (EMT) and Marxian monetary circuits establish that real-world banking operates in reverse causal sequence \citep{Kaldor1982,Moore1988,Lavoie2014}:
\begin{equation}
	\Delta \text{Bank Loans} \;\longrightarrow\; \Delta \text{Deposits} \;\longrightarrow\; \Delta \text{Central Bank Reserves} (H)
	\label{eq:endogenous_money_sequence}
\end{equation}
Commercial banks act as price-setters and quantity-takers in credit markets, extending working-capital loans to solvent firms to finance payrolls and production. These loans simultaneously create non-MFI bank deposits out of nothing. The central bank operates as an institutional lender of last resort, compelled to accommodate required reserves ex-post at the policy discount rate to maintain clearance in the interbank payments grid. Under EMT, the traditional money multiplier ($mm_t \equiv M1_t / H_t$) is not an exogenous causal driver but an unstable, ex-post accounting ratio reflecting commercial bank liquidity preferences, cash-drain ratios, and portfolio balance constraints ($\beta \neq 1, \alpha \ne 0$). In peripheral capitalism, credit supply is structuralist and upward-sloping \citep{Palley2013,Dow2006,Rochon1999}: commercial banks' willingness to intermediate credit is bounded by external liquidity constraints and foreign exchange exposure.

\textbf{3.11} This institutional reality establishes what I define as the \emph{Peripheral Paradox}. In core economies issuing world money, the central bank accommodates domestic credit without facing an absolute currency boundary. In the periphery, however, while domestic credit generation is naturally endogenous ($\text{Loans} \to M_1$), the ultimate means of international settlement is bound to an exogenously constrained reserve asset ($e_t \cdot IR_t$). The peripheral central bank must back an endogenously expanding domestic credit pyramid with an externally constrained stock of world money. When external credit embargoes, copper price collapses, or private capital flight drain foreign reserves, the state cannot act as an unconstrained lender of last resort in hard currency; it must choose between contracting domestic activity or suffering balance-sheet insolvency. Under the UP, the nationalized commercial banking system under the State Development Corporation (CORFO) extended defensive working-capital credit to socialized and private enterprises facing distribution bottlenecks and price controls, compelling the Central Bank to monetize enterprise deficits. Domestic money growth thus functioned as an endogenous accommodation valve, driven by structural cost-push pressures rather than discretionary printing press impulses.

\subsection{The Peripheral Central Bank Balance Sheet under International Financial Subordination}
\label{sec:B5_foley_chamorro_minsky}
\label{sec:foley_minsky_solvency}

\textbf{3.12} To formalize how this balance-sheet contradiction generates macroeconomic crisis, I reformulate the Financial Instability Hypothesis (FIH) of \citet{Minsky1986} for the peripheral central bank acting as the lender of last resort (LLR). In Minsky's canonical framework, financial fragility is endogenously generated during prolonged expansions: stability breeds instability as private firms and banks voluntarily increase leverage, transitioning from Hedge to Speculative and Ponzi postures \citep{Kregel2008}. However, Minsky formulated this taxonomy for private actors in a closed, metropolitan economy possessing complete monetary sovereignty, where an unconstrained central bank (the ``Big Bank'') and fiscal authority (the ``Big Government'') stabilize the system.

\textbf{3.13} Transposing this framework to a public central bank in the periphery requires resolving the five theoretical concerns raised by advisors regarding actor logic, profit motives, and exogeneity. First, regarding actor logic and private versus public debt, \citet[p.~21]{Foley2003} demonstrates in aggregate cash-flow accounting ($R + D = I + V$) that during acute financial fragility, the state and the central bank as LLR are compelled to absorb the illiquidity and insolvency of private enterprises onto their own balance sheets. The central bank does not lever up out of private profit-seeking or subjective greed; it does so by executing its institutional LLR mandate to prevent payments gridlock and economic breakdown. Second, regarding the objective function, the central bank's behavior is dictated by the structural balance-sheet identity of reserve solvency:
\begin{equation}
	\text{SolvR}_t \;\equiv\; \frac{e_t \cdot IR_t}{M_{1,t}}
	\label{eq:solvency_ratio}
\end{equation}
governed by the continuous dynamic law of motion:
\begin{equation}
	\Delta \text{SolvR}_t \;=\; \frac{CA_t + KA_t}{M_{1,t}} \;-\; \text{SolvR}_t \cdot g_{M,t}
	\label{eq:solvency_law_motion}
\end{equation}
where $CA_t$ is current account net receipts, $KA_t$ is net capital flows, and $g_{M,t}$ is domestic money growth.

\textbf{3.14} Third, regarding exogeneity, \citet{ChamorroFutinico2025} demonstrates that financial instability in peripheral Latin American economies operates as an \emph{externally amplified endogenous fragility}. Minsky's dynamic cannot be applied to dependent formations without recognizing that external shocks from the center---the collapse of world monetary arrangements, terms-of-trade declines, and credit embargos---serve as the primary triggers detonating domestic balance-sheet fragility. Fourth, regarding motivation, the peripheral central bank enters a \emph{Forced Ponzi} posture not out of speculative euphoria, but under severe institutional duress. When imperial sanctions cut off commercial trade credit, refusing domestic liquidity accommodation would cause immediate real-sector collapse and social starvation. Accommodating the deficits of socialized enterprises is a defensive imperative to maintain societal metabolism.

\textbf{3.15} Fifth, this reformulation provides the theoretical derivation for the three regimes identified in my empirical Threshold Vector Autoregression (TVAR):
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Hedge Central Bank ($\text{SolvR}_t > \hat{\gamma}_{S,1} = 0.21$):} Foreign reserve assets ($IR_t$) comfortably cover domestic liquid liabilities and several months of essential imports. The central bank possesses genuine policy autonomy: expanding domestic credit ($g_{M,t} > 0$) to reactivate idle capacity does not trigger exchange-rate panics because its external backing remains fully credible, yielding zero price pass-through ($\beta_{\text{Hedge}} \approx 0.00\%$).
	\item \textbf{Speculative Central Bank ($0.14 < \text{SolvR}_t \le 0.21$):} Current export earnings no longer cover external debt service and essential imports simultaneously ($CA_t < 0$). The central bank maintains external settlement by continuously rolling over short-term revolving trade credits ($KA_t > 0$). Reserve erosion strains credibility, generating moderate inflationary pass-through ($\beta_{\text{Spec}} = +0.54\%$).
	\item \textbf{Forced Ponzi Central Bank ($\text{SolvR}_t \le \hat{\gamma}_{S,2} = 0.14$):} Imperial sanctions unilaterally cancel revolving credit lines ($KA_t \ll 0$), driving liquid reserves below critical import minimums (\$30 million in July 1972). The central bank cannot meet international payment commitments out of current receipts or market borrowing, yet must emit domestic liquidity to prevent the collapse of socialized distribution. This unbacked domestic emission passes directly and discontinuously into hyperinflation ($\beta_{\text{Ponzi}} = +4.54\%$) and currency flight.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Four Structural Dimensions of the Unidad Popular Crisis}
\label{sec:B6_four_structural_dimensions}
\label{sec:four_structural_dimensions}

\textbf{3.16} Four interconnected structural shocks drove the descent of the Unidad Popular's Solvency Ratio: First, the August 15, 1971 Nixon Shock dismantled the Bretton Woods system, injecting global commodity inflation directly into Chilean import costs. Second, the secular decline of corporate profitability in the advanced capitalist core \citep{Weisskopf1979} motivated imperial retaliation against Chilean copper nationalization. The Nixon administration mobilized an invisible financial blockade that froze Export-Import Bank credits, multilateral loans, and commercial credit lines, precipitating an acute reserve drain.

\textbf{3.17} Third, this external chokehold opened a relational trade account scissors, defined by the divergence of two distinct economic domains. On the physical and real purchasing power domain, Prebisch-ECLAC Capacity to Import ($M^{\text{cap}}_t \equiv [P^{\text{Cu}}_t / P^{\text{Cu}}_{70:11} \times 100] \times [\text{Exports}_t / \text{Imports}^*_t]$) collapsed from $100.0$ to $27.7$ in August 1971, measuring the severe contraction in physical import purchasing power resulting from declining copper prices and the embargo on capital goods. This collapse blew open the Real Structural Imbalance ($\Theta_t \equiv M_t / M^{\text{cap}}_t \times 100$, formalizing the Latin American structuralist concept of \textit{desequilibrio estructural externo}) from $105.7$ in late 1970 to an unsustainable peak of $283.4$ in May 1973, identifying the physical structural imbalance that throttled domestic manufacturing and generated autonomous price pressure. Concurrently, on the nominal and exchange-rate domain, the Relative Price Disparity Index ($\Omega_t \equiv \frac{P^{\text{CL}}_t / P^{\text{CL}}_{70:11}}{(e_t / e_{70:11}) \cdot (P^{\text{US}}_{W,t} / P^{\text{US}}_{W,70:11})} \times 100 \approx 1/\text{RER}_t$) exploded from $100.0$ to $190.6$ in May 1973 under the official exchange rate peg ($e_t = 0.0122$). Because imperial credit embargoes enforced hard quantity rationing on physical imports in foreign currency, $\Omega_t$ diverged from effective import volumes, recording the domestic price disparity maintained by the state's ethical defense of the social wage.

\textbf{3.18} Fourth, domestic capitalist resistance compounded this external scissors. The October 1972 Bosses' Lockout (\textit{Paro Patronal}) and intermediate distribution sabotage paralyzed transport networks and hoarded essential commodities. To prevent industrial paralysis, the central bank was forced to act as lender of last resort to socialized enterprises and state distribution boards, expanding base money to fund basic payrolls. This defensive liquidity expansion crushed the Solvency Ratio below the Ponzi threshold ($\text{SolvR}_t \le 0.14$) by late 1972, igniting explosive hyperinflation under structural foreign-exchange exhaustion.

\textbf{4.1} Investigating the macroeconomic trajectory and ultimate breakdown of Chile under the Unidad Popular (UP, 1970--1973) requires an empirical foundation grounded in primary archival ledgers, institutional reporting, and cross-chapter harmonization. Secular annual series (1920--2010, $T=91$) anchor structural and balance-sheet relationships. Net physical capital stocks across five disaggregated asset categories (Machinery and Equipment, Non-Residential Construction, Residential Construction, Transport Equipment, and Other Assets), the aggregate rate of profit on productive assets ($r$), and structural capacity utilization ($\mu_t$) are integrated directly from the harmonized perpetual inventory dataset developed in Chapter~2 of this dissertation. Secular balance-of-payments accounts and net international investment positions (NIIP) are drawn from the World Balance of Payments Database \citep{NievasPiketty2025}. Finally, historical labor mobilization ledgers are compiled from primary records: agricultural grievance petitions (\textit{pliegos de peticiones}) from the Archivo de la \textit{Dirección General del Trabajo}, \citet{MorrisOyaneder1962}, \citet{Loveman1971,Loveman1976}, and \citet{BravoVargas2017}; industrial strike frequencies from Clio-Lab PUC \citep{DiazLudersWagner2016}; peasant union membership from \citet{Santana2006} and \citet{Loveman1976}; trade union affiliation and density from \citet{OsorioPolanco2021}; and urban squatter land seizures from \citet{Murphy2015}.

\subsubsection{Multi-Frequency Integration: Monthly vs. Secular Annual Panel}
\label{sec:data_panels}

\textbf{4.2} This data architecture resolves two fundamental econometric obstacles in peripheral macroeconomics: the \emph{temporal aggregation fallacy} and the \emph{closed-system endogeneity bias}. Standard macroeconomic narratives \citep{DornbuschEdwards1990,Edwards2024} rely almost exclusively on annual aggregates or stylized quarterly abstractions. Low-frequency data compress the chronological sequence of political and economic shocks, generating the optical illusion that domestic monetary emission was the exogenous prime mover of inflation and external insolvency. 

To overcome these distortions, this chapter deploys a synchronized multi-frequency research design coordinating two empirical tiers, following the applied econometrics reporting standards of UMass Amherst. The secular annual tier ($T=91$, 1920--2010) evaluates long-run capital accumulation, structural capacity utilization ($\mu_t$), and secular profit squeeze dynamics ($H_2$), where physical capital gestation spans multiple quarters and requires perpetual inventory balance-sheet accounting. In contrast, the monthly tier ($N=252$, 1960:01--1980:12) is indispensable for isolating causal ordering, dynamic balance-sheet transmission, and threshold non-linearities ($H_1$ and $H_3$). In an embattled peripheral open economy subject to financial blockades, reserve depletion and domestic credit accommodation unfold across weeks and months; annual temporal aggregation compresses this sequence and generates the spurious impression that domestic money creation preceded external asphyxiation. Full archival provenance, series codes, and transformation details for both empirical tiers are cataloged in Appendix~\ref{app:archival_codebook} (Table~\ref{tab:app_monthly_codebook}). The recursive SVAR event-study framework (1960--1973) evaluating parameter stability across discrete institutional shocks is documented in Appendix~\ref{app:annual_svar}, while the comprehensive atlas of high-frequency monthly TVAR Generalized Impulse Response Functions across solvency regimes (1960--1980) is reported in Appendix~\ref{app:annual_tvar}.

\textbf{4.3} In primary statistical repositories, index series are constructed with heterogeneous base years reflecting bureaucratic chaining cycles rather than economic logic. In the archival baseline constructed for this chapter, the Central Bank Consumer Price Index uses December 2008 as base 100, which, due to currency re-denominations, yields a nominal price level of $0.0012$ in November 1970. Historical physical volume indices for manufacturing and mining reflect disparate baselines of $80.56$ and $90.03$ in November 1970, while customs export and import indicators are historical volume ratios ($2.68$ and $1.51$).

Although \citet{DiazBahamonde2023} constructs a spliced monthly activity index with inverse-volatility weighting across four series, my research design explicitly demotes this composite aggregate. Collapsing structurally divergent sectors into a single weighted index conceals the fundamental real asymmetry of peripheral breakdown: consumer-goods manufacturing expanded by $33.3\%$ in 1971 through idle capacity reactivation, whereas capital-intensive mining was throttled by the external spare-parts blockade and grew only $7.0\%$. I therefore focus attention directly on the four disaggregated physical and real series: industrial manufacturing, mining production, real export activity, and real import activity.

\subsubsection{Trade Account Operationalization: Capacity to Import vs. Structural Price Disparity}
\label{sec:data_trade_indices}

\textbf{4.4} Following econometric event-study methodology \citep{MacKinlay1997,FreyaldenhovenEtAl2019}, I implement a dual-normalization protocol. First, for multi-regime trajectory comparisons, all physical production series and trade capacity indicators are re-based to November 1970 ($1970:11 = 100$), the inaugural benchmark of the UP government, so that every observation directly quantifies cumulative real expansion or contraction relative to inherited conditions. Second, in high-frequency event studies around discrete political shocks, series are normalized to $100$ at the immediate pre-event month ($t = -1$) to isolate the post-shock trajectory from prior drift. Third, series with invariant natural units---world copper prices in USD/lb, London gold in USD/oz, gross reserves in USD millions, official exchange rates in Escudos per dollar, and monthly inflation in percent---preserve their exact physical and financial dimensions.

To separate external purchasing power from domestic price distortions, I construct two complementary monthly trade series normalized to the inaugural UP benchmark ($1970:11 = 100$):
\begin{equation}
	M^{\text{cap}}_t \equiv \left( \frac{\text{Exports}_t}{\text{Exports}_{1970:11}} \right) \times \left[ \frac{P^{\text{Cu}}_t / P^{\text{Cu}}_{1970:11}}{P^{\text{US}}_{W, t} / P^{\text{US}}_{W, 1970:11}} \right] \times 100
	\label{eq:capacity_to_import}
\end{equation}
where $P^{\text{Cu}}_t$ is the London Metal Exchange copper price, $P^{\text{US}}_{W, t}$ is the U.S. Wholesale Price Index (\texttt{PPIACO}), and $\text{Exports}_t$ is the real customs export activity index sourced from \citet{DiazBahamonde2023}. $M^{\text{cap}}_t$ operationalizes the Prebisch-ECLAC purchasing power formula, quantifying the physical quantum of imports that Chile's export receipts could command at prevailing terms of trade. 

To measure the physical balance-of-payments pressure directly, I define the \emph{Real Structural Imbalance Ratio} ($\Theta_t$), operationalizing the Latin American structuralist concept of \textit{desequilibrio estructural externo} \citep{Prebisch1950,Furtado1964,Rodriguez1980}, as the percentage ratio of effective real customs imports ($M_t$, re-based to Nov 1970 = 100) to real import capacity:
\begin{equation}
	\Theta_t \equiv \left( \frac{M_t}{M^{\text{cap}}_t} \right) \times 100
	\label{eq:theta_imbalance}
\end{equation}
When $\Theta_t > 100\%$, physical import volumes exceed real export purchasing power, generating a structural foreign exchange deficit that must be financed either through foreign credit lines or by depleting Central Bank reserves. Under the imperial credit embargo and Nixon Shock in August 1971, $\Theta_t$ surged to $374.9\%$ (Table~\ref{tab:marglin_monthly_grounding}), meaning that import requirements were nearly four times export purchasing power, explaining why Central Bank international reserves plunged by $\$139.9$ million in the second half of 1971.

Complementing this real volume indicator, the \emph{Relative Price Disparity Index} ($\Omega_t$) measures the purchasing power parity wedge or domestic-to-imported cost ratio, constructed from the official nominal exchange rate ($e_t$) and the domestic price level ($P^{\text{CL}}_t$):
\begin{equation}
	\Omega_t \equiv \frac{P^{\text{CL}}_t / P^{\text{CL}}_{1970:11}}{\left( \frac{e_t}{e_{1970:11}} \right) \times \left( \frac{P^{\text{US}}_{W, t}}{P^{\text{US}}_{W, 1970:11}} \right)} \times 100 \equiv \frac{1}{\text{RER}^{\text{WPI}}_t}
	\label{eq:structural_imbalance}
\end{equation}
When $\Omega_t > 100$, domestic prices outrun the domestic-currency cost of imported wholesale goods, reflecting cumulative real currency overvaluation and pent-up rationing pressures. Because the state enforced strict price controls and pegged the exchange rate throughout 1971, $\Omega_t$ registered $98.2$ in December 1971, remaining completely blind to the external reserve drain. Reconciling the real physical scissors ($\Theta_t$) with the nominal price disparity ($\Omega_t$) resolves the identification of external shocks without contaminating the structural models with endogenous domestic inflation.

\subsubsection{Stationarity Diagnostics and Historical Structural Breaks}
\label{sec:data_stationarity}

\textbf{4.5} Before entering dynamic econometric models, all candidate macroeconomic and balance-of-payments variables were subjected to formal integration order testing, following the applied econometrics guidelines of UMass Amherst, documented fully in Appendix~\ref{app:unit_root_battery} (Table~\ref{tab:urca_results_full}). In time-series political economy, verifying stationarity is essential: entering non-stationary $I(1)$ series in levels without cointegration yields spurious regressions, artificial $t$-ratios, and non-stationary residuals that conceal structural misspecification. Valid model specification requires confirming that candidate variables are covariance-stationary---either as pure $I(0)$ processes, as stationary processes conditional on documented historical structural breaks, or transformed into stationary differences ($\Delta \ln X_t$)---before inclusion in VAR or TVAR architectures. Following four complementary unit root tests (ADF, DF-GLS, KPSS, and Zivot-Andrews), the candidate series satisfy these stationarity criteria.

These endogenous breaks capture decisive historical turning points in Chilean political economy. The structural break identified in 1934 in the relative agricultural-to-manufacturing productivity ratio reflects the institutional stabilization of the Import Substitution Industrialization (ISI) regime post-Great Depression: the Alessandri administration established comprehensive foreign exchange controls through the \textit{Comisión de Cambios Internacionales}, enacted protective tariffs, and stabilized relative agricultural terms to reconstruct domestic purchasing power. Similarly, the 1958 endogenous structural break in the aggregate wage share ($\omega$) marks the repeal of the \textit{Ley Maldita} (\textit{Ley de Defensa Permanente de la Democracia}), the re-legalization of the Communist Party, and the opening of the ``Long Sixties'' in Chilean social historiography. This political realignment shattered the decade-long wage suppression enforced under the 1948--1958 authoritarian regime, generating an institutional upward ratchet in labor share dynamics that set the structural stage for the distributive clash of the early 1970s.

\subsection{Historical Grounding: The Rise of the Unidad Popular (1934--1970)}
\label{sec:historical_grounding}

\textbf{4.6} Deciphering the long-run accumulation regime that preceded Salvador Allende's election requires synthesizing the \citet{Goodwin1967} distributive cycle with the \citet{Lewis1954} surplus labor framework within a peripheral, disarticulated economy. The political economy of the popular government was not an unprompted populist aberration, but the structural outcome of the exhaustion of the agrarian latent industrial reserve army under the institution of \textit{inquilinaje}. For decades, the institutionalized suppression of rural wages had subsidized urban manufacturing profitability. The breakdown of this disciplinary architecture opened the long cycle of tripartite social mobilization that culminated in the 1970 election.

\subsubsection{Agrarian Inquilinaje and the Latent Industrial Reserve Army (1931--1947)}
\label{sec:hist_inquilinaje}

\textbf{4.7} Deciphering the long-run accumulation regime that preceded the UP requires dismantling the dual-economy myth that dominates neoclassical modernization theories \citep{Lewis1954,HarrisTodaro1970}. In standard dualist models, the agrarian sector is treated as a pre-capitalist, feudal remnant characterized by zero marginal productivity of labor, which passively yields surplus population to modern industry until reaching a commercial turning point. As \citet{Frank1967} demonstrated in his foundational critique of Latin American dualism, the Chilean central-valley agricultural estate was never an autarkic feudal enclave; it had been organically integrated into world commodity circuits and urban merchant capital since the colonial era. Following \citet{Richards1979}, the agrarian oligopoly was defined by the landlord's undivided sovereignty over the peasant workforce under the social institution of \textit{inquilinaje}. The economic rationale of the agricultural estate was capitalist: treating labor as a variable cost, maximizing land rent and cash revenues, and minimizing cash outlays. The low material living standards and high labor intensity of the countryside reflected the strategy of agrarian capital operating under an institutionalized guarantee of captive, politically disenfranchised rural labor. With abundant labor and legally prohibited rural unions, landlords faced zero market incentives to substitute costly imported machinery for cheap manual labor.

Under the institutional settlement that stabilized the state following the Great Depression shock---codified in the 1931 Labor Code under Arturo Alessandri Palma and consolidated through the 1934 institutional stabilization of import-substituting industrialization (ISI)---the urban industrial bourgeoisie and the landed oligarchy forged a historic class compromise. The state legally recognized industrial enterprise trade unions in urban factories, establishing conciliation boards and collective bargaining mechanisms. In exchange, the state guaranteed that rural workers would remain legally unorganized and politically excluded. Within Marxian accumulation theory (\textit{Capital}, Vol.~1, Ch.~25), the institution of \textit{inquilinaje} allowed the countryside to function as an elastic latent industrial reserve army (\textit{ej\'ercito industrial de reserva latente}). By expelling rural surplus population toward expanding urban centers, the countryside provided an elastic supply of labor that structurally disciplined urban manufacturing workers, preventing real wages from tracking industrial productivity and widening the mass of surplus value available for domestic and foreign capital accumulation.

The stability of this disciplinary architecture was challenged whenever democratic openings weakened landlord authority. Following the electoral victory of Pedro Aguirre Cerda's Popular Front (\textit{Frente Popular}) in 1938, peasant workers mobilized across the Central Valley: registered agricultural labor petitions jumped from fewer than 4 per year between 1932 and 1938 to 171 in 1939 and 199 in 1940 \citep{MorrisOyaneder1962,Loveman1971,Loveman1976} (see the archival ledgers of rural labor mobilization in Appendix~\ref{app:historical_labor_ledgers}, Section~\ref{app:petitions_ledger}). Confronted by this mobilization, the landed oligarchy (\textit{Sociedad Nacional de Agricultura}, SNA) threatened an agricultural investment strike and food withholding, compelling Aguirre Cerda to issue a confidential circular in March 1940 ordering labor inspectors to freeze the legal constitution of peasant unions. The containment proved temporary. When post-WWII inflation sparked massive urban unrest, Radical Gabriel Gonz\'alez Videla was elected in September 1946 with official Communist Party support, appointing Communist ministers to his cabinet, including the Ministry of Agriculture. The presence of a Communist minister shattered landlord deference: \textit{inquilinos} struck for cash wages and security of tenure, driving agricultural labor petitions to an all-time historical zenith of 383 in 1947, representing 31.1\% of all labor disputes in Chile \citep{MorrisOyaneder1962,Loveman1976,BravoVargas2017}.

\subsubsection[The ``Ley Maldita'' and Coercive Restabilization]{The ``Ley Maldita'' as Coercive Restabilization of the Accumulation Regime (1948--1958)}
\label{sec:hist_leymaldita}

\textbf{4.8} Faced with the simultaneous breakdown of labor discipline in copper mines, coal fields, and agrarian estates, the ruling bloc responded with authoritarian violence. In September 1948, Congress promulgated Law 8,987 for the Permanent Defense of Democracy (\textit{la Ley Maldita}), outlawing the Communist Party, stripping over 25,000 workers of voting rights, purging trade union leaderships, and establishing the Pisagua internment camp. Simultaneously, Congress enacted Law 8,811 on Peasant Unionization, which imposed insurmountable legal barriers on rural organization by mandating at least 20 workers over 18 years old on the same estate, requiring at least 10 to be literate, and strictly banning strikes during harvest periods.

The empirical impact of this authoritarian counter-offensive was immediate and structural across all dimensions of the distributive nexus, as visually demonstrated in Figure~\ref{fig:goodwin_lewis_distributive_conflict}. First, agricultural collective petitions collapsed by 97.1\%, plunging from 383 in 1947 to 24 in 1948, 16 in 1949, and just 11 in 1950 \citep{MorrisOyaneder1962,Loveman1976,BravoVargas2017}. Second, as documented in the historical trade union density series compiled by \citet{OsorioPolanco2021} (see full archival ledger in Appendix~\ref{app:union_density_ledger}), total trade union membership was forcibly frozen. While the active workforce expanded by over 370,000 workers between 1947 and 1958, total union affiliates remained trapped between 260,000 and 284,000. Trade union density over employed workers declined steadily from 13.66\% in 1947 to 12.03\% in 1958, reaching a historical trough of 11.17\% in 1964. Third, by violently restoring rural labor discipline, the state reconstituted the countryside's role as a latent reserve army. As audited in \citet{Astorga2023}, the aggregate wage share ($\omega$) was crushed from 68.2\% in 1947 to 59.5\% in 1948, eventually bottoming out at 41.8\% in 1955 under the orthodox Klein-Saks mission. In response, the aggregate rate of profit on productive fixed assets surged from 4.47\% in 1947 to 6.52\% in 1950 and peaked at 10.64\% in 1955 (estimated from the Chapter~2 harmonized capital stock dataset), subsidized by the violent suppression of agrarian and urban wages. The repeal of the \textit{Ley Maldita} in 1958 and the re-legalization of the Communist Party marked the institutional threshold opening the ``Long Sixties'' in Chilean social historiography, setting the stage for the unravelling of this repressive equilibrium.

\subsubsection{Pseudo-Urbanization, the Stagnant Reserve Army, and the Tripartite Social Mobilization}
\label{sec:hist_tripartite}

\textbf{4.9} While the \textit{Ley Maldita} restored profitability in the short run, it exacerbated the underlying contradictions of peripheral industrialization. Because late peripheral ISI lacked a domestic capital-goods industry (Department I), it depended entirely on imported technology designed for the capital-labor ratios of advanced capitalist nations. Urban manufacturing suffered from a severe labor absorption bottleneck: the share of manufacturing employment in the total labor force remained virtually flat around 18\% throughout the massive rural exodus of 1940--1965. At the same time, monopolistic land tenure and low technical reinvestment caused an acute agrarian bottleneck: the ratio of agricultural to manufacturing labor productivity collapsed from 0.225 in 1940 to 0.130 in 1960 and 0.113 in 1970 \citep{DiazLudersWagner2016}. Displaced peasants expelled from the estates could not be absorbed into high-productivity industrial jobs. Out-migration from the countryside produced pseudo-urbanization and structural marginality, channeling expelled rural laborers into Marx's stagnant industrial reserve army (\textit{ej\'ercito industrial de reserva estancado}), swelling low-productivity tertiary informality and populating precarious peripheral settlements (\textit{poblaciones callampas}).

This structural contradiction was accelerated by the Latin American Developmental Welfare State (LADWS) \citep{IllanesRiesco2007}. The consolidation of the National Health Service (\textit{Servicio Nacional de Salud}, SNS) in 1952 sparked a nationwide sanitary revolution, dramatically reducing infant mortality and accelerating the demographic transition. Between 1940 and 1970, the urban share of the Chilean population surged from 52.5\% to over 75\% \citep{DiazLudersWagner2016}. Simultaneously, the expansion of social security, public education, and housing boards (CORVI) instituted a substantial social wage (\textit{salario indirecto}), raising the reservation wage of working-class households. By exhausting the pool of captive, docile agrarian laborers, this structural transition brought forward the Lewis Turning Point c. 1957, when the agricultural-industrial wage discount closed permanently \citep{RodriguezWeber2014}. 

With the exhaustion of the latent reserve army, class struggle underwent a profound spatio-temporal displacement from the rural estates to the urban periphery, triggering the tripartite social mobilization visualized in Panel~A of Figure~\ref{fig:goodwin_lewis_distributive_conflict}. Following Michal Kalecki's (1943) classical formulation of the \textit{political aspects of full employment}, the breakdown of reserve-army discipline emboldens the working class to challenge managerial authority. In Chile, this dynamic detonated across three fronts. First, the urban industrial working class radicalized collective action: total strikes surged from 259 in 1960 and 676 in 1964 to 1,853 in 1970, cresting at an all-time historical peak of 3,037 strikes in 1972 (involving over 542,800 workers), with illegal and wildcat strikes accounting for an astonishing 96.1\% of all disputes \citep{MorrisOyaneder1962,DiazLudersWagner2016}. Second, the agrarian working class mobilized en masse following the Christian Democratic agrarian reforms of Eduardo Frei Montalva (the 1967 Peasant Unionization Law 16,625 and Agrarian Reform Law 16,640), which shattered landlord sovereignty over \textit{inquilinos}. Peasant union membership exploded from a negligible 1,652 workers in 1964 to 143,142 in 1970, 232,160 in 1971, and 250,740 in 1973 \citep{Santana2006,Loveman1976}. Third, the urban squatter movement of \textit{pobladores} seized the city to assert their right to social reproduction: as documented by \citet{Murphy2015} (see ledger in Appendix~\ref{app:land_seizures_ledger}), illegal urban land seizures (\textit{tomas de terreno}) in Santiago surged from 13 in 1967 to 23 in 1969, jumped to 103 in 1970, and exploded to 350 in 1971, alongside 560 nationwide seizures in 1970. The \textit{pobladores} established autonomous territorial command structures that operated beyond traditional clientelism, forging the militant urban base that propelled the Left to power.

\subsubsection{The Marxian Profit Squeeze, the Investment Strike, and Accumulation Collapse (1960--1975)}
\label{sec:hist_profit_squeeze}

\textbf{4.10} The tripartite mobilization of organized labor, peasant federations, and urban squatters collided directly with the macroeconomic prerequisites of capitalist valorization. In Marxian accumulation theory, the expansion of the working-class wage share beyond the growth of labor productivity induces an acute profit squeeze, compressing the mass and rate of surplus value available for capital reproduction. In the peripheral Chilean setting, this distributive clash was directly mediated by the structure of productive fixed capital. To trace the macroeconomic consequences of this clash on capitalist reproduction, I evaluate the aggregate rate of profit ($r$) on productive fixed assets. Following the capital accounting methodology established in Chapter~2, capital stock ($K$) and investment ($I$) correspond strictly to productive fixed assets---Machinery and Equipment ($ME$) plus Non-Residential Construction ($NRC$)---while strictly excluding Residential Construction ($RC$) as social overhead infrastructure and durable consumption. 

To isolate the structural transmission of the profit squeeze, I decompose the aggregate rate of profit using the classical Weisskopf-Marxian formulation:
\begin{equation}
	r_t \equiv \frac{\Pi_t}{P_{K, t} K_t} = (1 - \omega_t) \cdot \frac{Y_t}{P_{K, t} K_t} = (1 - \omega_t) \cdot \frac{Y_t}{Y^p_t} \cdot \frac{Y^p_t}{P_{K, t} K_t} = (1 - \omega_t) \cdot \mu_t \cdot \sigma_t
	\label{eq:weisskopf_decomp}
\end{equation}
where $\omega_t$ is the functional wage share (so $1 - \omega_t$ represents the profit share or rate of surplus value), $\mu_t \equiv Y_t / Y^p_t$ is the structural rate of capacity utilization estimated via the Cointegrating Multivariate Polynomial Regression (CMPR IM-OLS) framework of Chapter~2, and $\sigma_t \equiv Y^p_t / (P_{K, t} K_t)$ is potential capital productivity (the capacity output-capital ratio at replacement cost). 

In accordance with perpetual inventory balance-sheet accounting, net capital accumulation is discounted from the implicit annual physical depreciation of net stocks ($D_t \equiv K_{t-1} + I_t - K_t$, with depreciation rate $\delta_t \equiv D_t / K_{t-1}$). Net accumulation is therefore linked directly to profitability through the classical Cambridge-Marxian identity:
\begin{equation}
	g^n_{K, t} \equiv \frac{I_t - D_t}{K_{t-1}} = \chi_t \cdot r_t = \chi_t \cdot (1 - \omega_t) \cdot \mu_t \cdot \sigma_t
	\label{eq:cambridge_accum_identity}
\end{equation}
where $\chi_t \equiv I^{\text{net}}_t / \Pi_t$ represents the re-capitalization propensity or accumulation rate of the capitalist class out of surplus value $\Pi_t \equiv (1 - \omega_t) Y_t$ (complete mathematical derivations and balance-sheet proofs are documented in Appendix~\ref{app:profit_accumulation_ledger}).

Tracing this balance-sheet trajectory granularly year by year illuminates the structural mechanisms:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{1968 (Frei Fiscal Austerity and Drought):} Following the agrarian reform mobilization and a severe agricultural drought, late-Frei wage suppression held the wage share at $\omega = 53.94\%$ and open unemployment at $u = 4.36\%$. Capacity utilization stood at $\mu = 93.66\%$, potential capital productivity at $\sigma = 0.2618$, and the aggregate profit rate at $r = 11.29\%$, sustaining a net productive accumulation rate of $g^n_K = 3.96\%$ with capitalist re-capitalization propensity $\chi = 0.337$.
	\item \textbf{1969 (Pre-Electoral Wage Compression and Military Unrest):} Wage compression intensified prior to the 1970 election, pushing the wage share down to $\omega = 50.05\%$ (profit share $49.95\%$) while unemployment rose to $u = 4.96\%$. Amidst growing political polarization and the \textit{Tacnazo} garrison revolt, the aggregate profit rate crested at $r = 12.79\%$, sustaining net accumulation of $g^n_K = 3.82\%$ ($\chi = 0.288$).
	\item \textbf{1970 (Inherited Baseline at Allende's Election):} In 1970, Salvador Allende inherited an aggregate profit rate of $12.23\%$ and an implicit depreciation rate of $\delta = 7.40\%$, supporting a net productive accumulation rate of $g^n_K = 4.23\%$ ($\chi = 0.332$), underpinned by late-Frei wage compression ($\omega = 50.93\%$), open unemployment of $u = 5.90\%$, and structural capacity utilization at $\mu = 92.78\%$ ($\sigma = 0.2685$).
	\item \textbf{1971 (Keynesian-Kaleckian Reactivation Peak):} Through statutory wage readjustments and price controls, the UP pushed the aggregate wage share to $\omega = 56.23\%$, compressing the profit share to $43.77\%$, while open unemployment fell to $u = 5.20\%$. Surging physical capacity utilization to its all-time historical peak of $\mu = 98.44\%$ temporarily cushioned profitability ($r = 10.62\%$) despite the falling profit share. However, capital reproduction was already decelerating: re-capitalization propensity dropped to $\chi = 0.304$, slowing net capital accumulation to $g^n_K = 3.34\%$.
	\item \textbf{1972 (Distributive Peak and Profit Squeeze Crisis):} The statutory wage offensive reached its zenith as the wage share surged to an unprecedented historical peak of $\omega = 69.03\%$ and open unemployment fell to its historical minimum of $u = 4.00\%$. In immediate response, the aggregate profit rate suffered a severe compression, falling by nearly half to $r = 6.57\%$ (a collapse of $46.3\%$ relative to 1970). Because physical depreciation absorbed $\delta = 5.78\%$ of the capital stock, domestic capitalists responded with an investment strike: re-capitalization propensity contracted to $\chi = 0.277$, plunging net productive accumulation to $g^n_K = 1.85\%$. This valorization deadlock precipitated the October 1972 nationwide employers' lockout (\textit{Paro de Octubre}).
	\item \textbf{1973 (Input-Asphyxiation and Capital Reproduction Paralysis):} As the U.S. financial blockade choked off imported machinery replacement parts, industrial input-asphyxiation pulled physical capacity utilization down to $\mu = 89.46\%$. Facing expropriation threats and vanishing cash returns, private capital deepened its investment boycott: re-capitalization propensity collapsed to $\chi = 0.184$, dragging net productive capital accumulation to a near-standstill of $g^n_K = 1.71\%$. The September 11, 1973 military coup d'état was the violent political mechanism deployed to break this valorization deadlock.
	\item \textbf{1974 (Dictatorial Wage Repression):} By outlawing trade unions, freezing wages, and deregulating prices under Decree Law 522, the Pinochet dictatorship crushed the wage share down to $\omega = 39.45\%$ (restoring the profit share to $60.55\%$), which forcibly revived the aggregate profit rate to $r = 10.67\%$ and slightly lifted net accumulation to $g^n_K = 2.38\%$ ($\chi = 0.218$), even as open unemployment jumped to $u = 9.10\%$.
	\item \textbf{1975 (Chicago Shock Therapy Depression):} The imposition of orthodox fiscal and monetary shock therapy caused open unemployment to explode to $u = 18.00\%$ and crashed physical capacity utilization to a depression trough of $\mu = 76.62\%$. Despite a high nominal profit share ($50.73\%$), domestic demand destruction collapsed the actual profit rate back to $r = 6.54\%$ and depressed net productive accumulation to an all-time low of $g^n_K = 1.21\%$ ($\chi = 0.183$).
\end{itemize}


\subsection[Macroeconomic Trajectories and Balance-of-Payments Accounting (1970--1973)]{Macroeconomic Trajectories and Balance-of-Payments\\ Accounting (1970--1973)}

\subsubsection{Balance-of-Payments Forensic Accounting (1970--1973)}
\label{sec:data_bop_forensic}

\textbf{4.11} Before specifying econometric models or evaluating competing causal hypotheses, I ground the empirical inquiry in the physical, financial, and institutional reality of the 1,000 days of the Unidad Popular (November 1970 -- September 1973). The fundamental material limit governing peripheral socialist transition is the external balance-of-payments frontier. In standard national accounting, the annual change in official Central Bank international reserves ($\Delta IR_t$) satisfies the exact balance-of-payments identity:
\begin{equation}
	\Delta IR_t \equiv CA_t + KA_t + EO_t + SDR_t
	\label{eq:bop_reserves_identity}
\end{equation}
where $CA_t$ represents the current account balance, $KA_t$ denotes net capital and financial account inflows, $EO_t$ captures net errors and omissions (reflecting unrecorded private capital flight and trade misinvoicing), and $SDR_t$ denotes allocations of IMF Special Drawing Rights.

\input{tables/tab00_annual_bop_reserves_accounting.tex}

The audited forensic accounting in Table~\ref{tab:bop_reserves_accounting} establishes the four balance-of-payments mechanisms governing the external collapse. First, the reserve buffer inherited in November 1970 (\$408.0 million) rested on debt-financed liquidity: under the Christian Democratic administration of Eduardo Frei Montalva (1964--1970), persistent current account deficits averaging $-\$100$ million annually were systematically over-financed by U.S. Alliance for Progress loans and foreign mining credits ($+\$168.0$ million in 1966 and $+\$267.5$ million in 1970). This left external solvency acutely vulnerable to foreign financing withdrawals. Second, the reserves collapse of 1971 ($\Delta IR = -\$299.8$ million) was driven primarily by the sudden reversal of the capital account: net external financing collapsed from $+\$267.5$ million in 1970 to $-\$26.5$ million in 1971, representing a net contraction of $-\$294.0$ million in a single calendar year and accounting for $98.1\%$ of the total reserve drain. Following the U.S. Export-Import Bank credit denial of August 12, 1971 and multilateral loan freezes, private foreign commercial banks slashed short-term revolving trade credit from $\$220$ million to $\$35$ million, forcing cash-in-advance payments for essential imports and draining foreign liquidity. Third, unrecorded private capital flight and trade misinvoicing captured in Net Errors and Omissions generated an additional drain of $-\$84.5$ million in 1971 and $-\$171.6$ million in 1972 (totaling $-\$389.0$ million across the UP triennium), as domestic elites extracted liquid assets abroad. Fourth, while official capital inflows statistically rebounded to $+\$327.4$ million in 1972, this represented an accounting entry reflecting the unilateral debt moratorium declared in November 1971, which recorded unpaid debt service as exceptional financing while liquid foreign exchange fell below $\$30$ million.

\subsubsection{The External Chokehold: Rescuing and Breaking with De Vylder through the Nixon Shock}
\label{sec:theory_devylder_nixon}
\label{sec:devylder_nixon_shock}

\textbf{4.15} A foundational debate in the political economy of the Unidad Popular concerns the relative causal weight of domestic economic management versus external imperial intervention. In standard neoclassical and monetarist accounts \citep{DornbuschEdwards1990,Edwards2024}, the breakdown is attributed entirely to internal ``macroeconomic populism''---excessive fiscal deficits and unconstrained monetary printing. In contrast, conventional left-wing narratives \citep{Bitar1979} reduce the crisis to bilateral U.S. sanctions and CIA covert actions.

My core empirical and theoretical contribution is to reframe this debate through the lens of the dependency research programme as formulated by \citet{Kvangraven2021}. Following Lakatosian epistemology, Kvangraven defines the hard core of dependency theory by its relational ontology: peripheral macroeconomic dynamics cannot be analyzed as an insular closed system, but must be understood as structurally conditioned by the peripheral economy's subordinate location within the global division of labor and the international monetary hierarchy. In this light, my analysis operates a two-stage theoretical maneuver: rescuing the structural insights of \citet{DeVylder1976}, while executing a decisive break with his causal conclusion by integrating the exogenous global shock of the August 15, 1971 convertibility break (the Nixon Shock).

Against simplistic monetarist narratives, I rescue \citet{DeVylder1976}'s seminal insight that the Chilean crisis was fundamentally structural. De Vylder correctly identified that the critical bottleneck of the Chilean economy lay in \emph{input-asphyxiation} (\textit{asfixia por insumos}) stemming from technological dependence on the United States, far outweighing any shortfall in selling copper on world markets. In manufacturing and mining, more than 90\% of capital equipment was imported, and over 95\% of capital goods in the large copper mines (\textit{Gran Miner\'ia}) originated from the United States \citep[pp.~17, 129]{DeVylder1976}. At the same time, De Vylder demonstrated that the redistributive wage policy produced an inescapable \emph{import-composition squeeze}: surging popular consumption forced the state to divert scarce foreign exchange from industrial capital goods into basic food imports (\$165 million in 1970 to \$468 million in 1972; \citealt[p.~103]{DeVylder1976}), crowding out replacement parts, intermediate alloys, and industrial machinery.

Despite these structural insights, \citet{DeVylder1976} made an analytical concession that ultimately capitulated to the orthodox domestic-blame narrative. Concluding his balance-of-payments analysis, De Vylder asserted:
\begin{quote}
\small \textit{``The problem was the insatiable imports, not the US blockade nor the lack of credit. The total volume of credits---and of imports---increased markedly, and if the quantity and composition of Chilean imports turned out to be inadequate it was due to the domestic economic situation.''} \citep[p.~106]{DeVylder1976}
\end{quote}
This assertion has been repeatedly weaponized by orthodox scholars \citep{Edwards2024} to argue that even sympathetic socialist observers admitted the blockade was irrelevant. I break decisively with De Vylder's conclusion, showing that it rests on two severe empirical and theoretical blindspots: the neglect of \emph{credit non-fungibility} in the international currency hierarchy, and above all, the total omission of the \emph{August 15, 1971 Nixon Shock}.

De Vylder's claim that import demand was ``insatiable'' treats the dollar import bill as an endogenous reflection of domestic physical demand. In doing so, De Vylder completely omitted the systemic global monetary rupture that occurred on August 15, 1971, when President Richard Nixon unilaterally closed the gold window and dissolved the Bretton Woods par-value anchor. As the audited series demonstrate (Table~\ref{tab:marglin_monthly_grounding} and Figure~\ref{fig:copper_gold}), the London gold price exploded from \$37.44/oz to \$106.76/oz by August 1973, while the U.S. Wholesale Price Index ($P^{\text{US}}_{W,t}$) escalated by $+28.0\%$ (rising from $100.0$ to $128.0$). This signaled a worldwide wave of primary commodity stagflation: world prices of basic imported wage-goods---wheat, corn, edible oils, meat, and crude oil---doubled and tripled in international dollar spot markets. 

Because the Chilean state maintained a fixed nominal exchange rate ($e_t = 0.0122$) to shield working-class consumption while world wholesale prices soared, this exogenous shock blew open the Relative Price Disparity Index ($\Omega_t$) from $100.0$ to a peak of $190.6$ in May 1973 (Figure~\ref{fig:import_capacity}), while the physical Real Structural Imbalance ($\Theta_t \equiv M_t / M^{\text{cap}}_t \times 100$) escalated to $283.4$. Imported goods were rendered artificially cheap in domestic Escudos, intensifying domestic queues and black-market arbitrage while devouring Central Bank foreign exchange reserves. The escalating import bill reflected an exogenous multiplication of world dollar prices for essential wage-goods as the core hegemon exported domestic inflation to the periphery. De Vylder's volume-based critique conflated an exogenous international price and terms-of-trade rupture with domestic consumption excess.

De Vylder further argued that ``the total volume of credit increased'', citing bilateral credit lines extended by the Soviet Union, Eastern Europe, and Western Europe. However, through the lens of \citet{Kvangraven2021} and the international currency hierarchy, this argument commits a category mistake by ignoring credit non-fungibility. Credits extended by the socialist bloc were tied to specific Eastern European machinery and long-gestation industrial projects; they could neither be converted into liquid convertible foreign exchange to purchase grain on spot markets, nor could they be used to purchase U.S.-patented spare parts for Chile's American-built mining equipment and transport fleet (>95\% U.S.-sourced; \citealt{DeVylder1976}). Meanwhile, private U.S. commercial banks slashed short-term revolving trade credit lines---the liquid lifeblood of international commerce---from \$220 million to \$35 million. Because Western exporters demanded 100\% cash in advance, the Central Bank was forced to exhaust its liquid reserves (\$140M lost by August 1971, Table~\ref{tab:marglin_monthly_grounding}) simply to maintain basic nutritional inflows. Nominal gross credit totals completely obscured liquid convertible currency starvation.

Viewing fiat money as a credit-debt relationship between the Central Bank and the population within an international currency hierarchy \citep{Kvangraven2021}, domestic liabilities require external asset backing to anchor prices. When the Nixon Shock and credit blockade severed this anchor and drained foreign exchange reserves, the UP faced private distribution withholding and an acute U.S. spare-parts embargo. To prevent mass urban starvation and factory closures, the Central Bank was forced into an externally induced, defensive credit posture: emitting domestic credit money against an asset backing that was being actively extinguished by foreign sanctions and elite capital flight. Domestic price acceleration was the socialized expression of an externally strangled Central Bank balance sheet pushed into structural insolvency.

The structural imbalance was relational \citep{Kvangraven2021}, generated by the collision between the government's commitment to defend working-class real consumption through a pegged official exchange rate ($e_t = 0.0122$) and the imperial credit blockade combined with post-Nixon Shock global commodity stagflation ($P^{\text{US}}_{W,t} +28.0\%$). This price wedge opened the trade account scissors, operating simultaneously as a nominal disparity ($\Omega_t = 190.6$) and a physical volume asphyxiation ($\Theta_t = 283.4$ vs. $M^{\text{cap}}_t = 27.7$), rendering imports artificially cheap in domestic currency and subsidizing consumption at the expense of foreign reserves. Confronted with an imperial siege, the popular government rejected orthodox recessionary austerity.

By integrating the Nixon Shock, currency hierarchy, and credit-money ontology into De Vylder's input-asphyxiation framework, my analysis accomplishes what \citet{Kvangraven2021} terms a \emph{progressive problem shift}. It resolves the anomalies that led De Vylder to retreat into domestic-blame concessions, while providing a rigorous, non-reductionist account of peripheral breakdown. The collapse of the Unidad Popular was the tragic result of a peripheral socialist transition colliding with the systemic crisis and unilateral restructuring of global dollar hegemony.

\subsection{Competing Theoretical Frameworks: Exogenous Money vs. Endogenous Accommodation}
\label{sec:tournament_theory}
\label{sec:tournament_design}

\subsubsection{Three Competing Paradigms and Causal Directed Acyclic Graphs}
\label{sec:theory_dags}

\textbf{4.16} In empirical macroeconomic research, testing competing paradigms requires establishing operational decision rules capable of discriminating between rival causal structures. Under the scientific research programme framework of \citet{Lakatos1978} and the hypothesis tournament design of \citet{KingSznajder2006}, discriminating between orthodox and heterodox paradigms requires testing the hard-core causal postulates that separate money exogeneity from endogenous monetary accommodation \citep{Kaldor1982,Moore1988,Rochon1999}. In the Latin American peripheral setting, the foundational debate concerning the breakdown of the Unidad Popular is defined by an analogous, yet structurally deeper, paradigm cleavage. 

On one side, the orthodox tradition---encompassing both neoclassical Monetarism ($H_1$, \citealp{DornbuschEdwards1990,Edwards2024}) and Austrian Business Cycle Theory ($H_2$, \citealp{EspinosaVejar2025})---rests upon the hard-core axiom of money supply exogeneity. In this view, monetary and credit expansion is conceptualized as an autonomous, policy-driven prime mover ($g_M \to \pi$ or $g_M \to \Delta K$), where the Central Bank operates as an unconstrained domestic printing press with zero reverse feedback running from price inflation, wage demands, or balance-of-payments bottlenecks back into monetary emission ($\pi \not\to g_M$). On the other side, the Post-Keynesian and Latin American Structural-Dependency tradition ($H_3$, \citealp{CardosoFaletto1979,Kvangraven2021,ClavijoCortesKappesRochon2025}) conceptualizes fiat credit money as an endogenous social and institutional liability anchored by foreign exchange within an international currency hierarchy. In this structuralist ontology, monetary emission is an endogenous accommodation valve drawn forward defensively by external balance-of-payments asphyxiation, terms-of-trade collapses, and domestic distributive conflict under rigid supply bottlenecks.

\subsubsection{Frequency Differentiation and Testable Zero Restrictions}
\label{sec:theory_restrictions}

\textbf{4.17} In translating these theoretical paradigms into econometric tests, a critical methodological consideration emerges regarding data frequency and the operational collapse of rival hypotheses. In historical accounts, Monetarism ($H_1$) and Austrian ABCT ($H_2$) are often treated as distinct traditions: Monetarism emphasizes rapid price-level pass-through driven by excess cash balances, whereas Austrian theory emphasizes relative price distortions, credit-induced malinvestment in roundabout production processes ($\Delta K \gg 0$), and subsequent capital goods collapse. However, on a high-frequency monthly panel, the historical Chilean statistical apparatus provides no measures of gross fixed capital formation, capital goods deflators, or roundabout inventory structures. At the monthly frequency, $H_1$ and $H_2$ collapse into an identical, unified testable restriction: \emph{Money Supply Exogeneity} ($H_0: \pi_{t-k} \not\to g_{M,t}$). Both orthodox accounts require that credit and base money expansion operates as an uncaused initiating shock, asserting that prior inflation has zero predictive power over monetary emission. 

Conversely, the Austrian malinvestment mechanism can only be discriminated from structuralist capacity reactivation at an annual frequency, where audited perpetual inventory series for physical capital stocks exist. Austrian ABCT demands that monetary expansion induce a physical over-investment boom in higher-order capital goods (Machinery and Equipment, $I_{\text{ME}}$, and Non-Residential Construction, $I_{\text{NRC}}$). In contrast, the structural-dependency thesis predicts an idle capacity reactivation in popular consumption goods with physical investment stagnation ($\Delta K \le 0$) due to the external spare-parts embargo. Figure~\ref{fig:causal_dags} formalizes these competing causal mechanisms via Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAGs), while Table~\ref{tab:tournament_restrictions} establishes the zero restrictions and empirical decision rules governing the comparative model evaluation. \subsection{Econometric Methodology: A Four-Stage Testing Strategy}
\label{sec:econometric_methodology}

\textbf{4.18} To evaluate the competing hypotheses with empirical rigor, I establish a coordinated multi-stage econometric procedure designed to test directional precedence, physical capital accumulation, and non-linear balance-sheet thresholds \citep{Sims1972,Lutkepohl2005}. While canonical tests of endogenous money evaluate linear vector autoregressions, peripheral economies facing severe external constraints require extending this framework to account for non-linear balance-sheet thresholds. The empirical procedure comprises four sequential stages: (1) high-frequency Granger causality and Wald tests to evaluate directional precedence between money, prices, and sectoral output; (2) annual perpetual inventory physical capital stock accounting and capacity utilization tests to evaluate Austrian roundabout malinvestment against structuralist capacity reactivation; (3) identification of the structural contemporaneous causal ordering of a Multivariate Threshold Vector Autoregressive model (\texttt{tsDyn::TVAR}) via empirical Granger temporal precedence across Minskian solvency regimes; and (4) high-frequency event studies around discrete historical turning points combined with recursive horizon parameter estimation and non-parametric causal discovery.

\subsubsection{Stage 1: High-Frequency Directional Precedence and Reverse Accommodation}
\label{sec:meth_stage1}

\textbf{4.19} The first econometric stage evaluates the direction of temporal precedence between price inflation and base money growth on the monthly panel (1960:01--1980:12, $N=251$). Following \citet{Granger1969} and \citet{Sims1972}, testing for causal precedence does not imply philosophical causation in an absolute sense, but examines whether past values of a regressor contain statistically informative content for predicting future values of a response variable beyond its own autoregressive history. I specify the bivariate autoregressive system in first differences to ensure covariance stationarity:
\begin{align}
\Delta g_{H,t} &= \alpha_{0} + \sum_{k=1}^p \alpha_{k} \Delta g_{H,t-k} + \sum_{k=1}^p \beta_{k} \Delta \pi_{t-k} + \varepsilon_{H,t} \label{eq:granger_money} \\
\Delta \pi_t &= \delta_{0} + \sum_{k=1}^p \delta_{k} \Delta \pi_{t-k} + \sum_{k=1}^p \gamma_{k} \Delta g_{H,t-k} + \varepsilon_{\pi,t} \label{eq:granger_price}
\end{align}
where $\Delta g_{H,t}$ denotes the month-over-month growth of Central Bank base money, $\Delta \pi_t$ is the change in monthly consumer inflation, and $p \in \{1, 2, 3, 4\}$ denotes the lag order selected via Akaike (AIC) and Schwarz Bayesian (BIC) information criteria. 

The orthodox exogeneity hypothesis ($H_1$ and $H_2$) imposes the strict restriction that base money growth is uncaused by prior price dynamics, establishing the null hypothesis $H_0^{(A)}: \beta_1 = \dots = \beta_p = 0$. Conversely, the endogenous accommodation hypothesis ($H_3$) posits that Central Bank emission responded defensively to prior inflation and supply bottlenecks, requiring the rejection of $H_0^{(A)}$ and the statistical dominance of reverse accommodation over forward pass-through ($H_0^{(B)}: \gamma_1 = \dots = \gamma_p = 0$). To evaluate these restrictions, I report both the finite-sample $F$-statistic and the asymptotic Wald $\chi^2$ statistic across lag lengths $p \in \{1, \dots, 4\}$. I apply the identical specification to test sectoral output responses ($\Delta g_{H} \to \Delta g_{\text{Manuf}}$ vs. $\Delta g_{H} \to \Delta g_{\text{Mining}}$), testing whether monetary emission stimulated consumer goods reactivation or capital-intensive extraction.

To eliminate pre-test biases associated with unit root and cointegration testing, I complement the differenced system with the augmented levels VAR framework of \citet{TodaYamamoto1995}. Setting the lag order $k \ge d_{\max}$ ($k=2$ based on AIC/BIC) and incorporating the maximal suspected integration order $d_{\max}=1$ from Appendix~\ref{app:unit_root_battery}, I estimate an over-fitted levels VAR with $p = k + d_{\max} = 3$ lags:
\begin{equation}
	Y_t = \mathbf{c} + \sum_{i=1}^k \mathbf{\Phi}_i Y_{t-i} + \sum_{j=1}^{d_{\max}} \mathbf{\Phi}_{k+j} Y_{t-k-j} + \varepsilon_t
	\label{eq:toda_yamamoto_spec}
\end{equation}
The Wald restriction test for directional non-causality is applied strictly to the first $k$ coefficient matrices ($\mathbf{\Phi}_1, \dots, \mathbf{\Phi}_k$), leaving the final $d_{\max}$ lag coefficients unconstrained to absorb non-stationary stochastic trends. This guarantees standard asymptotic $\chi^2$ distributions under arbitrary integration orders, satisfying the degrees-of-freedom condition $T_{\text{effective}} > n \cdot (k + d_{\max}) + c_{\text{deterministic}}$ ($248 > 2 \cdot 3 + 2$).

\subsubsection{Stage 2: Physical Capital Stock Accounting and Roundabout Investment Testing}
\label{sec:meth_stage2}

\textbf{4.20} The second econometric stage discriminates Austrian Business Cycle Theory ($H_2$) from Structural-Dependency idle capacity reactivation ($H_3$). Because high-frequency monthly series cannot observe capital goods formation, evaluating Austrian claims of credit-induced malinvestment requires auditing physical capital accumulation on the annual panel (1960--1975) through the perpetual inventory method (GPIM; see Chapter~2 dataset). ABCT predicts that artificial credit expansion lengthens the structure of production by inducing an unsustainable over-investment boom in higher-order, capital-intensive assets: Machinery and Equipment ($I_{\text{ME}}$) and Non-Residential Construction ($I_{\text{NRC}}$). In contrast, the structuralist framework posits that monetary accommodation financed the utilization of pre-existing idle capacity in wage-goods manufacturing, while physical capital accumulation stagnated or contracted ($\Delta K \le 0$) due to the U.S. embargo on capital replacement parts and foreign exchange insolvency. The empirical decision rule compares real investment trajectories ($\Delta I_{\text{ME}}, \Delta I_{\text{NRC}}$) against structural capacity utilization ($\mu_t$), testing whether the 1971 output expansion reflected physical capital deepening or the intensive utilization of inherited industrial capacity.

\subsubsection[Stage 3: Multivariate Threshold Vector Autoregressions and Solvency Regimes]{Stage 3: Multivariate Threshold Vector Autoregressions\\ and Solvency Regimes}
\label{sec:meth_stage3}

\textbf{4.21} The third econometric stage captures the state-dependent, non-linear transmission mechanism of $H_3$ by specifying a Multivariate Threshold Vector Autoregressive model (TVAR) estimated via the CRAN package \texttt{tsDyn} \citep{Stigler2019}. Methodologically, standard time-series identification confronts the trilemma diagnosed by \citet{Runge2019}: pairwise Granger causality tests suffer from bivariate naivety by omitting common drivers, whereas saturated VAR specifications with all lags of all variables succumb to degrees-of-freedom exhaustion and collider bias. To overcome this arbitrariness, the empirical architecture specifies the Real Structural Imbalance ($\Delta \ln \Theta_t \equiv \Delta \ln [M_t / M^{\text{cap}}_t \times 100]$) directly in the endogenous TVAR vector, as non-parametrically certified through the Peter-Clark Momentary Conditional Independence (PCMCI) causal discovery algorithm \citep{Runge2019} in Appendix~\ref{app:causal_pcmci}.

Grounded in empirical directional precedence and the non-parametrically discovered Directed Acyclic Graph (Appendix~\ref{app:causal_pcmci}), the empirical estimates establish four structural facts: (1) London gold market price growth ($g_{P,\text{gold},t}$) is strictly block-exogenous to Chilean domestic aggregates ($F = 0.817, p = 0.557$ from base money; $F = 1.328, p = 0.242$ from inflation); (2) real structural imbalance shocks directly Granger-cause consumer price acceleration ($\Delta \ln \Theta \to \Delta \pi$, $F = 5.113, p = 0.0246$) and throttle manufacturing output ($\Delta \ln \Theta \to g_{\text{Manuf}}$, $F = 4.364, p = 0.0378$); (3) this physical chokehold precedes domestic price acceleration, which in turn Granger-causes monetary issuance ($F = 28.324, p < 0.0001$); and (4) Central Bank emission operates as an accommodating valve lagging price shocks by three months, while displaying zero predictive power on gold prices ($p = 0.557$) or the structural imbalance ($F = 0.899, p = 0.4081$).

This empirical precedence uniquely identifies the structural Cholesky causal ordering of the four-variable endogenous vector:
\begin{equation}
y_t = \big[ g_{P,\text{gold},t}, \; \Delta \ln \Theta_t, \; \Delta \pi_t, \; \Delta g_{H,t} \big]'
\label{eq:cholesky_ordering}
\end{equation}
In structural matrix terms, the dynamic system satisfies $\mathbf{A}_0 y_t = \mathbf{c} + \sum_{j=1}^p \mathbf{A}_j y_{t-j} + \varepsilon_t$, with orthogonal structural innovations $\varepsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \mathbf{D})$ where $\mathbf{D} = \text{diag}(\sigma_1^2, \dots, \sigma_4^2)$ is strictly diagonal. Contemporaneous structural interactions are governed by the lower-triangular matrix:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{A}_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ -a_{21} & 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -a_{31} & -a_{32} & 1 & 0 \\ -a_{41} & -a_{42} & -a_{43} & 1 \end{bmatrix}
\label{eq:tvar_a0_matrix}
\end{equation}
This lower-triangular specification dictates that: (1) global world-money shocks contemporaneously affect the real structural imbalance, consumer prices, and Central Bank emission ($a_{21}, a_{31}, a_{41} \ne 0$), but are unaffected contemporaneously by domestic Chilean aggregates ($a_{12} = a_{13} = a_{14} = 0$); (2) real structural imbalance shocks contemporaneously affect domestic inflation and monetary requirements ($a_{32}, a_{42} \ne 0$), while being predetermined with respect to domestic prices and money ($a_{23} = a_{24} = 0$); (3) domestic price shocks contemporaneously compel Central Bank credit accommodation ($a_{43} \ne 0$), reflecting the lender-of-last-resort mandate to refinance wages, distribution networks, and state enterprises; and (4) Central Bank emission shocks cannot alter the domestic price level contemporaneously within the same month ($a_{34} = 0$).

The contemporaneous zero restrictions ($a_{12}=a_{13}=a_{14}=a_{23}=a_{24}=a_{34}=0$) and the diagonal covariance matrix $\mathbf{D}$ are not arbitrary conventions imposed by the researcher; they are the exact empirical properties verified by the non-parametric Momentary Conditional Independence (MCI) tests of the Peter-Clark Momentary Conditional Independence (PCMCI) causal discovery algorithm \citep{Runge2019} detailed in Appendix~\ref{app:causal_pcmci} (Table~\ref{tab:pcmci_monthly_gate} and Figure~\ref{fig:dag_pcmci_monthly_gate}). Methodologically, PCMCI establishes conditional independence by testing:
\begin{equation}
\text{MCI}: \quad X^i_{t-\tau} \;\perp\!\!\!\perp\; X^j_t \;\big|\; \hat{\mathcal{P}}(X^j_t) \setminus \{X^i_{t-\tau}\}, \; \hat{\mathcal{P}}(X^i_{t-\tau})
\label{eq:pcmci_mci_test_sec4}
\end{equation}
conditioning simultaneously on the minimal conditioning parents of the target $\hat{\mathcal{P}}(X^j_t)$ and the parents of the driver $\hat{\mathcal{P}}(X^i_{t-\tau})$. This protocol removes both time-series autocorrelation and contemporaneous common-driver confounding. In macroeconomic causal discovery, a recurring concern is time-aggregation bias: monthly sampling could conceal intra-month price-wage-money adjustments, creating spurious instantaneous links or masking the true causal direction. Runge's \citeyearpar{Runge2019} MCI framework explicitly inoculates the identification against time aggregation: by conditioning on both parent sets $\hat{\mathcal{P}}(X^j_t)$ and $\hat{\mathcal{P}}(X^i_{t-\tau})$, MCI controls for persistent unobserved intra-period feedback and filters out high-frequency autocorrelation. Under Faithfulness and Markov Completeness, if intra-month bidirectional feedback were operative, the MCI test would detect bidirectional or non-acyclic edges in the contemporaneous phase. Instead, all upper-triangular links are decisively rejected ($p > 0.10$), confirming that the monthly aggregation interval preserves the underlying recursive structural hierarchy. By the Causal Markov Condition \citep{Pearl2000,Spirtes2000,Runge2019}, once the minimal conditioning parent sets are controlled for, the structural innovations $\varepsilon_t = \mathbf{A}_0^{-1} u_t$ are mutually conditionally independent, guaranteeing that $\mathbf{D} \equiv E[\varepsilon_t \varepsilon_t']$ is strictly diagonal ($E[\varepsilon_{i,t} \varepsilon_{j,t}] = 0$ for all $i \ne j$). The recursive Cholesky factorization $\mathbf{\Sigma} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{P}'$ (where $\mathbf{P} = \mathbf{A}_0^{-1}\mathbf{D}^{1/2}$) therefore represents the unique data-certified identification of structural shocks, resolving the classical critique of atheoretical ordering arbitrariness \citep{CooleyLeRoy1985}. For robustness, I also estimate the alternative monetarist Cholesky ordering $y_t^{\text{alt}} = [ g_{P,\text{gold},t}, \; \Delta \ln \Theta_t, \; \Delta g_{H,t}, \; \Delta \pi_t ]'$ to verify that my core regime-switching findings are invariant to contemporaneous factorization.

Following \citet{Hansen1999} and \citet{ClavijoCortesKappesRochon2025}, the high-frequency monthly TVAR is formulated as:
\begin{equation}
y_t = \sum_{k=1}^K \left[ \Pi_{0,k} + \sum_{j=1}^p \Pi_{j,k} y_{t-j} + \varepsilon_{t,k} \right] I\{\gamma_{k-1} < \text{SolvR}_{t-d} \le \gamma_k\}
\label{eq:tvar_system}
\end{equation}
where the endogenous vector is $y_t = [g_{P,\text{gold},t}, \Delta \ln \Theta_t, \Delta \pi_t, \Delta g_{H,t}]'$,\footnote{A methodological note on frequency and filter selection is warranted. Following the standard time-series principles established by \citet{Hamilton1994} and \citet{KilianLutkepohl2017}, the monthly TVAR is estimated on stationary first differences ($\Delta \pi_t, \Delta g_{H,t}$) rather than rolling 12-month year-over-year (YoY) rates. Overlapping annual moving sums $\pi_t^{\text{YoY}} = \sum_{j=0}^{11} \pi_{t-j}^{\text{MoM}}$ act as a linear filter that mechanically induces an artificial $\text{MA}(11)$ error autocorrelation into the VAR innovations, violating the white-noise assumption, generating spurious persistence, and smearing discrete monthly turning points (such as the August 1971 Nixon Shock and July 1972 reserve exhaustion) across a rolling 12-month window. Orthodox monetarist claims regarding lag structures and pass-through invariance \citep{Edwards2024} are evaluated comprehensively across the complete monthly impulse response atlas in Appendix~\ref{app:annual_tvar}.} while $\text{SolvR}_{t-d} \equiv \frac{e_{t-d} \cdot IR_{t-d}}{M1_{t-d}}$ is the lagged Central Bank Solvency Ratio. The delay parameter is set to $d=1$ ($\text{SolvR}_{t-1}$). Setting $d \ge 1$ enforces a predetermined state transition, ruling out contemporaneous simultaneity between structural innovations in month $t$ and regime switches, and ensuring that the regime threshold indicator is strictly predetermined with respect to the structural shock vector $\varepsilon_t$. Here $K \in \{1, 2, 3\}$ allows nested testing of a three-regime specification against two regimes and a linear VAR null, $\Pi_{0,k}$ and $\Pi_{j,k}$ are regime-specific parameter matrices, and $\varepsilon_{t,k} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma_k)$ captures regime-specific heteroscedastic covariance matrices. 

The threshold variable $\text{SolvR}$ operationalizes the transposition of Minsky's financial postures to the Central Bank balance sheet \citep{Foley2003,ChamorroFutinico2025}: Hedge ($\text{SolvR} > \gamma_2$), Speculative ($\gamma_1 < \text{SolvR} \le \gamma_2$), and Forced Ponzi ($\text{SolvR} \le \gamma_1$). Beyond this theoretical foundation, selecting $\text{SolvR}$ as the state-dependent gateway is validated by the non-parametric PCMCI causal discovery algorithm in Appendix~\ref{app:causal_pcmci}: $\text{SolvR}$ emerges as the primary state-dependent parent gating domestic inflation ($\Delta \text{SolvR}_{t-3} \to \pi_t, -0.128, p = 0.0497$, Table~\ref{tab:pcmci_monthly_gate}). Linearity is formally evaluated using \citet{Hansen1999}'s sequential SupLR test statistics based on 1,000 bootstrap replications with a 15\% trimming parameter ($\epsilon = 0.15$). Dynamic transmission across regimes is evaluated using Generalized Impulse Response Functions (GIRF) with 68\% bootstrap confidence bands, tracing how shocks to world gold prices, the real structural imbalance, and base money growth propagate into inflation across alternative solvency states.

Estimating the system at monthly frequency is essential to preserve the discrete institutional timing of the crisis. Chilean political economy between 1970 and 1973 was punctuated by sharp, high-frequency shocks: the August 1971 Nixon Shock and multilateral credit freeze; the July 1972 exhaustion of liquid foreign reserves below \$30 million; the October 1972 employers' distribution lockout (\textit{Paro de Octubre}); and the September 1973 coup d'\'etat accompanied by Decree Law 522 releasing repressed prices. A rolling twelve-month year-over-year filter mechanically smears each discrete monthly shock across the subsequent twelve months, obscuring regime transitions and creating the optical illusion of an unmediated monetary lag. 

A compelling historical test of this unmediated lag hypothesis is provided by the macroeconomic trajectory of 1971. Between November 1970 and December 1971, Central Bank base money expanded by over 100\%. Yet monthly inflation remained quiescent at 1.5--2.0\%, and annual inflation actually declined from 35.0\% in 1970 to 22.1\% in 1971. Inflation accelerated into triple digits only after July 1972, the exact month when foreign exchange reserves breached the critical Ponzi threshold ($\text{SolvR}_t \le 0.14$) and import capacity collapsed. The high-frequency TVAR explicitly separates this reserve-backed expansion from the subsequent balance-of-payments asphyxiation.

\subsubsection{Stage 4: High-Frequency Event Windows, Recursive SVAR, and Causal Discovery}
\label{sec:meth_stage4}

\textbf{4.22} The fourth econometric stage complements the structural TVAR with high-frequency event studies around discrete institutional turning points and recursive parameter estimation. Event study windows span three months prior to and three months following each discrete shock ($t \in [-3, +3]$), normalizing all series to 100 at the immediate pre-event month ($t=-1$) to isolate post-shock dynamics from background trends \citep{MacKinlay1997}. Concurrently, to evaluate whether parameter instability reflected external balance-of-payments shocks or domestic policy shifts, the structural VAR is estimated recursively from an initial baseline of $N=143$ (January 1960 to December 1971) and expanded sequentially across the four historical junctures: (1) December 1971 (peak Keynesian reactivation, $N=143$); (2) September 1972 (post-Nixon Shock and credit freeze, $N=152$); (3) August 1973 (post-lockout supply paralysis, $N=163$); and (4) December 1973 (post-coup price deregulation, $N=167$). 

The recursive SVAR on $\mathbf{Y}_t = [\Delta \ln \Theta_t, g_{\text{Manuf},t}, g_{M0,t}, \pi_t]'$ shares this identical non-parametric PCMCI DAG identification (Appendix~\ref{app:annual_svar}). Specifically, external structural trade imbalance ($\Delta \ln \Theta_t$) is predetermined to all domestic variables ($a_{12}=a_{13}=a_{14}=0$); industrial manufacturing output ($g_{\text{Manuf},t}$) responds contemporaneously to import bottlenecks ($a_{21} \ne 0$) while being predetermined with respect to domestic base money and consumer prices ($a_{23}=a_{24}=0$); Central Bank base money ($g_{M0,t}$) accommodates industrial credit requirements ($a_{32} \ne 0$) without same-month feedback from consumer prices ($a_{34}=0$); and consumer inflation ($\pi_t$) absorbs contemporaneous shocks from all prior system variables ($a_{41}, a_{42}, a_{43} \ne 0$). Mutual innovation orthogonality ($\mathbf{D} = \text{diag}(\sigma_1^2, \dots, \sigma_4^2)$) is mathematically guaranteed by the Causal Markov Condition applied to the discovered DAG skeleton. This topological foundation provides a data-certified, parsimonious identification scheme that evaluates parameter stability and statistical significance across historical junctures without vulnerability to atheoretical ordering conventions.

\subsubsection{Stage 1 Results: Directional Precedence and Reverse Accommodation}
\label{sec:res_stage1}

\textbf{4.23} Table~\ref{tab:bachurewicz_granger_battery} reports the high-frequency Granger causality and directional precedence battery on the monthly panel (1960:01--1980:12, $N=250$) across lag lengths $\tau \in \{1, \dots, 4\}$. The empirical estimates deliver a decisive, threefold falsification of the Monetarist exogeneity axiom ($H_1$). 

First, reverse monetary accommodation decisively dominates exogenous monetary issuance across all lag lengths: the null hypothesis of zero accommodation ($\pi \to g_H = 0$) is rejected at the $p < 0.0001$ level across all specifications, with $F$-statistics reaching $28.324$ at lag 1, $18.515$ at lag 2, $20.318$ at lag 3, and $15.004$ at lag 4. Third, cross-correlation profiles reveal that monetary emission lagged price shocks by three months ($\text{Corr}(g_{H,t}, \pi_{t-3}) = +0.584$). The Central Bank expanded emission ex-post to accommodate prior cost-push pressures resulting from statutory wage increases, supply bottlenecks, and soaring world import prices, absorbing exogenous cost and supply shocks to prevent productive collapse. This directed precedence reflects the structural balance-sheet sequence of real-world banking ($\Delta \text{Bank Loans} \to \Delta \text{Deposits} \to \Delta H$). In 1971 and 1972, as price controls and transport bottlenecks squeezed enterprise liquidity, commercial banks extended working-capital credit to state-owned and private firms to maintain payrolls and distribution chains. The Central Bank was compelled to accommodate reserves ex-post to maintain interbank clearance, directly refuting the Fractional Reserve Theory sequence ($H \to \text{Deposits} \to \text{Loans} \to P$) and demonstrating empirical multiplier breakdown ($\beta \neq 1, \alpha \ne 0$).

Panel~C of Table~\ref{tab:bachurewicz_granger_battery} provides the empirical foundation for my four-variable structural ordering, differentiating the physical trade constraint from nominal price wedges. London gold price growth ($g_{P,\text{gold}}$) is confirmed to be strictly block-exogenous to the domestic Chilean economy: neither Central Bank base money ($F = 0.817, p = 0.557$) nor consumer inflation ($F = 1.328, p = 0.242$) Granger-causes world gold prices. Second, the Real Structural Imbalance ($\Delta \ln \Theta_t \equiv \Delta \ln [M_t / M^{\text{cap}}_t \times 100]$) is likewise strictly block-exogenous to domestic aggregates: neither base money emission ($F = 0.899, p = 0.4081$) nor domestic inflation ($F = 1.761, p = 0.1375$) Granger-causes physical structural imbalance, refuting the monetarist assertion that domestic money creation caused the external reserve hemorrhage. In contrast, physical structural imbalance shocks directly Granger-cause consumer price acceleration ($\Delta \ln \Theta \to \Delta \pi$, $F = 5.113, p = 0.0246$) and causally throttle manufacturing production ($\Delta \ln \Theta \to g_{\text{Manuf}}$, $F = 4.364, p = 0.0378$). Concurrently, the post-1971 Bretton Woods gold surge Granger-causes the widening of the nominal Relative Price Disparity Index $\Delta \ln \Omega_t$ ($F = 3.842, p = 0.0227$), transmitting world dollar stagflation into the domestic cost structure ($\Omega \to \pi$, $F = 2.974, p = 0.0070$), which in turn compels Central Bank credit accommodation ($F = 28.324, p < 0.0001$). The complete empirical chain confirms that external trade asphyxiation and structural cost wedges preceded monetary expansion. This directed sequence is corroborated non-parametrically by the PCMCI conditional independence tests in Appendix~\ref{app:causal_pcmci} (Table~\ref{tab:pcmci_monthly_gate}): import capacity shocks causally drive solvency depletion ($+0.384, p < 0.0001$) and inflation ($-0.298, p = 0.0008$), while base money growth displays zero predictive power over import capacity ($F = 0.000, p = 0.9962$ at lag 1; partial correlation $+0.018, p = 0.774$) or external solvency ($-0.045, p = 0.482$), decisively refuting the Monetarist DAG arrow.

\subsubsection{Stage 3 Results: Nonlinear Solvency Regimes and Inflation Jumps}
\label{sec:res_stage3}

\textbf{4.25} Having established the empirical Granger precedence identifying my structural ordering, I turn to the non-linear regime-switching estimates of the four-variable TVAR system (\ref{eq:tvar_system}) estimated via \texttt{tsDyn} \citep{Stigler2019}. Testing for threshold non-linearity confronts the \citet{Davies1977,Davies1987} nuisance parameter problem: under the null hypothesis of linearity ($H_0: \mathbf{\Phi}_1 = \mathbf{\Phi}_2, \mathbf{c}_1 = \mathbf{c}_2$), the threshold parameter $\gamma$ is not identified. Standard Wald and Likelihood Ratio statistics fail to follow standard asymptotic $\chi^2$ distributions. To conduct valid inference, I compute supremum Likelihood Ratio statistics ($\text{Sup-}LR = \sup_{\gamma \in \Gamma} LR(\gamma)$) over a trimmed parameter grid $\Gamma = [\tau, 1-\tau]$ with trimming parameter $\tau = 0.15$. Asymptotic $p$-values are generated using \citet{Hansen1996,Hansen1999}'s residual bootstrap ($B = 1,000$ replications). Methodologically, the effective sample size in each regime satisfies the degrees-of-freedom requirement $T_j \ge k \cdot p + c_{\text{deterministic}} + 1$ and well exceeds the asymptotic breakdown threshold ($T_j \ge 40$): the Hedge regime contains $T_1 = 143$ months ($57.2\%$), the Speculative regime contains $T_2 = 62$ months ($24.8\%$), and the Forced Ponzi regime contains $T_3 = 45$ months ($18.0\%$), ruling out small-sample parametric degeneration. A foundational methodological distinction separates static linear within-regime IRFs from non-linear Generalized Impulse Response Functions (GIRF). Computing standard Wold-representation linear IRFs within each regime while holding the regime indicator permanently fixed is strictly an intermediate diagnostic; it assumes the economy cannot transition endogenously across balance-sheet thresholds over the projection horizon. To capture genuine non-linear dynamics, I implement the history-dependent GIRF framework of \citet{KoopPesaranPotter1996}:
\begin{equation}
	\text{GIRF}_Y(h, \delta, \Omega_{t-1}) = \mathbb{E}[Y_{t+h} \mid u_t = \delta, \Omega_{t-1}] - \mathbb{E}[Y_{t+h} \mid \Omega_{t-1}]
	\label{eq:girf_definition}
\end{equation}
where $h=12$ months, $\delta$ is the structural innovation vector ($+1\sigma$), and $\Omega_{t-1}$ is the historical conditioning state. Future shocks are integrated out across $R = 500$ Monte Carlo bootstrap replications, averaging across historical dates belonging to each Minskyan regime ($\frac{1}{T_j} \sum_{t \in \text{Regime } j} \text{GIRF}_Y(h, \delta, \Omega_{t-1})$). This formulation allows structural shocks to endogenously alter the probability of crossing the solvency threshold $P(\text{SolvR}_{t+h-1} \le \gamma \mid u_t = \delta, \Omega_{t-1})$.

Figure~\ref{fig:girf_passthrough_3regimes} isolates the core empirical findings of the threshold monetary dynamics across the two foundational theoretical axes: Panel~A evaluates forward monetary pass-through ($\Delta g_H \to \Delta \pi$), while Panel~B evaluates reverse monetary accommodation ($\Delta \pi \to \Delta g_H$). Table~\ref{tab:girf_regime_differences} reports the companion formal tests of cumulative differences \citep{Afonso2018}. Dynamic trajectories are evaluated across four criteria \citep{RodriguezAsensio2026}: (1)~statistical significance via bootstrap containment against the horizontal zero reference line, (2)~horizon timing ($h$) of peak impact, (3)~persistence and convergence speed, and (4)~translation into concrete percentage-point scales. Specifically, in both the Hedge regime ($\text{SolvR} > 0.21$) and Speculative regime ($0.14 < \text{SolvR} \le 0.21$), the 95\% bootstrap credibility envelope spans the zero reference line across all twelve horizons ($h \in [0, 12]$), confirming that base money expansion produces statistically zero consumer price acceleration when foreign exchange backing is preserved. In stark contrast, under the Forced Ponzi regime ($\text{SolvR} \le 0.14$), the 68\% and 95\% credibility envelopes strictly exclude zero at early horizons, establishing immediate and statistically significant inflationary pass-through. Cumulative difference tests across regimes (Table~\ref{tab:girf_regime_differences}) corroborate this non-linear divergence at $p = 0.002$ ($h=3$) and $p = 0.0001$ ($h=8$), while Panel~B demonstrates that reverse monetary accommodation ($\Delta \pi \to \Delta g_H$) is statistically significant only under balance-sheet insolvency.

\subsection{The Political Economy of Peripheral Solvency and Subordinated Money}
\label{sec:discussion_solvency}

\textbf{5.1} The econometric findings in Section~\ref{sec:empirical_tournament} dismantle the foundational premise shared by orthodox interpretations of the Unidad Popular: the assumption that money growth is an unconstrained, exogenous policy choice. By showing that inflation and currency depreciation systematically preceded base money expansion ($\pi_{t-1} \to g_{M,t}$), and that the inflationary pass-through of monetary growth is strictly state-dependent on central bank reserve solvency ($\text{SolvR}_t$), my analysis opens the ``black box'' of peripheral monetary transmission. Central Bank money creation operated as the institutional accommodation valve through which the monetary authority, acting as lender of last resort (LLR), mediated the collision between domestic distributive commitments and an externally enforced foreign-exchange blockade.

\textbf{5.2} In a peripheral capitalist economy, the central bank operates under what I define as the \emph{Peripheral Paradox} (Section~\ref{sec:money_endogeneity_paradox}). While the domestic credit pyramid is naturally endogenous---commercial lending creates deposits, which the central bank must accommodate to prevent liquidity gridlock---the ultimate means of international settlement is exogenously rationed by access to world money ($e_t \cdot IR_t$). When external commercial credit lines are severed and copper receipts are tied up in litigation, the peripheral central bank cannot freely print international purchasing power. As reserves drain, the domestic monetary base ($H_t$) becomes structurally decoupled from foreign exchange backing. The central bank is placed in an impossible institutional bind: either it refuses liquidity to state-owned enterprises and private firms facing import cost explosions, triggering immediate mass bankruptcies and physical output collapse; or it acts as the lender of last resort, injecting emergency liquidity that, in the absence of foreign reserves to anchor the currency, accelerates domestic hyperinflation. The descent of the central bank's own balance sheet into a Forced Ponzi regime ($\text{SolvR}_m \le 0.14$), transposing the Minsky-Foley framework \citep{Foley2003,ChamorroFutinico2025} to peripheral central bank solvency, registered the structural exhaustion of peripheral solvency under external financial subordination.

\textbf{5.3} This institutional reality exposes the flaw in the orthodox treatment of retail shortages, rationing, and black markets. In the monetarist narrative of \citet{Edwards2024}, shortages are treated as unambiguous evidence of ``repressed inflation'' caused by price controls. In structural causal terms, however, retail shortages ($C$) constitute an \emph{endogenous collider variable} \citep{Pearl2009} caused jointly by wage-led demand stimulus ($U_1$) and private wholesale distribution sabotage, trucking strikes, and hoarding ($U_2$):
\begin{equation}
U_1 \longrightarrow [C] \longleftarrow U_2 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad U_1 \not\perp\!\!\!\perp U_2 \mid C
\end{equation}
By conditioning on empty store shelves without modeling the October 1972 Bosses' Lockout (\textit{Paro de Octubre}) or the deliberate withholding of inventories by the wholesale distribution monopoly (SDI), orthodox accounts commit an M-bias collider stratification error. They mistake the physical symptoms of organized class struggle and distribution sabotage for the mechanical consequences of central bank balance sheets, generating a powerful causal illusion that conceals the political economy of destabilization \citep{BlancoMatute2018,Casals2021}.

\subsection{The Unidad Popular in the Global Structural Crisis of the 1970s}
\label{sec:discussion_global_crisis}

\textbf{5.4} The crisis of the Unidad Popular cannot be understood in national isolation. It was the peripheral expression of a global structural crisis of capitalism that unfolded across the early 1970s. The long postwar boom in the advanced capitalist core was exhausting itself: corporate profit rates in the United States and Western Europe suffered a protracted secular decline \citep{Weisskopf1979,BowlesGordonWeisskopf1983}, labor militancy intensified, and the institutional foundations of Atlantic Fordism began to fracture. When President Richard Nixon closed the gold window on August 15, 1971, he unilaterally dissolved the gold-dollar convertibility anchor of the Bretton Woods system. This hegemonic shock exported U.S. stagflation directly to the periphery while reconfiguring the international currency hierarchy.

\textbf{5.5} For Chile, the timing was catastrophic. The Vuskovic economic program had been designed in late 1970 under the explicit assumption of a stable, fixed-exchange-rate international monetary regime backed by ample inherited reserves. Nine months into the administration, the institutional anchor of world money vanished. U.S. inflation drove up the dollar prices of Chilean imports, while the Nixon administration's directive to ``make the economy scream'' mobilized multilateral lending freezes (World Bank, IDB, Eximbank) and private commercial credit boycotts. The hegemonic state, facing its own domestic profitability crisis, deployed external financial subordination as an offensive instrument of imperial discipline against a peripheral nation exercising sovereignty over its natural resources \citep{Harmer2011,Arboleda2023}. The Unidad Popular fell when its redistributive commitments collided directly with the hard boundaries of the international currency hierarchy during an acute global structural crisis.

\subsection{Policy Implications for Contemporary Peripheral Sovereignty}
\label{sec:discussion_policy_implications}

\textbf{5.6} This historical reassessment yields fundamental analytical and policy lessons for contemporary peripheral formations attempting progressive or socialist developmental transitions. The Chilean experience provides a definitive critique of naive extensions of Modern Monetary Theory (MMT) to the global South. In an asymmetric international monetary hierarchy, sovereign authority over domestic currency issue does not confer command over world money \citep{Vasudevan2009,AlamiEtAl2022}. A peripheral government cannot simply finance developmental programs through central bank monetization without confronting the structural ceiling of foreign-exchange reserve solvency. 

\textbf{5.7} Peripheral monetary autonomy is strictly conditioned by the width of the external corridor. Sustaining developmental transitions requires active macroprudential management of foreign exchange, including mandatory surrender of export revenues, differentiated exchange-rate bands, and permanent, counter-cyclical capital controls to insulate domestic policy from international liquidity cycles \citep{FfrenchDavis2018,Vasudevan2019}. High nominal commodity export revenues are also insufficient to secure development if the physical composition of imports is vulnerable to external leverage. Peripheral planning must prioritize strategic self-sufficiency in non-substitutable capital equipment, replacement parts, and domestic food security to prevent foreign-exchange blockades from paralyzing the domestic productive apparatus.


\subsection{Concluding Synthesis: Toward a Progressive Research Programme}
\label{sec:concluding_synthesis}

\textbf{5.8} For more than fifty years, the historiography of the Unidad Popular has operated as an ideological weapon---a cautionary tale constructed by monetarist and Austrian orthodoxy to declare the impossibility of socialist transformation. By subjecting these claims to a formal Neo-Lakatosian Hypothesis Tournament, this chapter demonstrates that the orthodox narrative collapses under econometric scrutiny. First, the Monetarist hypothesis ($H_1$) fails: prior price shocks unidirectionally forced emergency monetary accommodation on the central bank balance sheet. Second, the Austrian ABCT framework ($H_2$) fails: the 1971 output surge represented a Keynesian-Structuralist reactivation of idle industrial capacity ($u = 98.4\%$) alongside contracting fixed capital formation ($\Delta K = -2.3\%$), ruling out credit-driven malinvestment. Third, the Structural-Dependency thesis ($H_3$) holds: the inflationary pass-through of monetization is strictly governed by central bank solvency thresholds ($\text{SolvR}_t$). Domestic liquidity is absorbed without inflation when reserves are solvent ($\text{IRF}_\pi = 0.00\%$), but detonates into hyperinflation once imperial blockades and reserve exhaustion push the central bank balance sheet into the Forced Ponzi regime (+4.54\% at $h=0$).

\textbf{5.9} Combining the relational thickness of a canonical case study with regime-switching econometrics, this chapter advances a \emph{progressive problem shift} within the dependency research programme \citep{Kvangraven2021,KingSznajder2006}. The analytical framework formalized here---the Solvency Ratio threshold model, the multi-frequency tournament architecture, and the decomposition of import composition constraints---provides a generative toolkit for analyzing peripheral financial subordination across historical and contemporary settings. From the structural crises of the Southern Cone in the 1970s and 1980s (Argentina, Peru) to contemporary debt traps in the global South, the central bank balance sheet remains the contested terrain where external financial subordination enters domestic reproduction. Reclaiming sovereignty in the periphery requires understanding that the struggle for economic democracy cannot stop at the borders of the nation-state; it must confront and transform the hierarchical architecture of the world monetary system.

\section[Formalization of Solvency Ratio and Balance-of-Payments Accounting]{Formalization of the Central Bank Solvency Ratio and\\Balance-of-Payments Accounting}
\label{app:bop_levr}

\textbf{A.1} This appendix develops the formal balance-sheet accounting, the exact discrete law of motion, and the structural constraint-binding conditions governing the central bank \textbf{Solvency Ratio} ($\text{SolvR}_t$). Every accounting step is an exact discrete identity; no linear approximation or continuous-time distortion enters the derivation.

\subsection{The Solvency Ratio in Nominal Terms}
\label{app:solvr_nominal}

\textbf{A.2} All variables are denominated in current domestic currency (Chilean pesos/escudos, CLP) at time $t$, with the nominal exchange rate convention defined as $e_t$ (CLP per USD), such that a nominal depreciation corresponds to $e_t \uparrow$.

\textbf{A.3} The balance-sheet and external-account variables are defined as follows:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em]
	\item $M_t$: domestic monetary obligations (CLP), representing the liquid liabilities of the consolidated domestic financial system underwritten by the central bank.
	\item $IR_t$: stock of gross international reserves (USD), representing claims denominated in world money.
	\item $e_t$: nominal exchange rate (CLP per USD).
	\item $F_t \equiv e_t \cdot IR_t$: stock of world money held by the central bank, valued in domestic currency (CLP).
	\item $\text{BoP}_t \equiv \Delta F_t$: net balance-of-payments flow (CLP).
	\item $g_{M,t} \equiv \Delta M_t / M_t$: simple growth rate of domestic money obligations.
	\item $g_{F,t} \equiv \Delta F_t / F_t = \text{BoP}_t / (e_t \cdot IR_t)$: simple growth rate of the world-money asset base.
	\item $X_t$: export volume of primary and manufactured goods.
	\item $Q_t$: import volume of intermediate, consumption, and capital goods.
	\item $P_{Q,t}^{USD}$: import price index in foreign currency (USD).
	\item $\text{ToT}_t \equiv P_{X,t}^{USD} / P_{Q,t}^{USD}$: terms of trade.
	\item $D_t^{ext}$: stock of external debt (USD).
	\item $r_t$: average nominal interest rate on external debt.
	\item $FDI_t$: net foreign direct investment inflows (USD).
	\item $\Pi_t$: net foreign profit and dividend remittances (USD).
	\item $T_t$: net current external transfers (CLP).
\end{itemize}

Import volumes carry the symbol $Q_t$ throughout to prevent notation collisions with domestic money $M_t$. Discrete growth rates are evaluated on the contemporaneous base to maintain algebraic exactness.

\textbf{A.4} I define the central bank Solvency Ratio ($\text{SolvR}_t$) in nominal terms as:
\begin{equation}
	\label{app:eq:solvr_def}
	\text{SolvR}_t \;\equiv\; \frac{e_t \cdot IR_t}{M_t} \;=\; \frac{F_t}{M_t}
\end{equation}
The Solvency Ratio measures the stock of world-money assets held by the central bank per unit of unanchored domestic credit obligations underwritten by the monetary authority. Its algebraic inverse, $\text{LevR}_t \equiv 1/\text{SolvR}_t = M_t / (e_t \cdot IR_t)$, expresses the corresponding balance-sheet leverage ratio. I center the analytical framework on the Solvency Ratio because it directly formalizes central bank reserve backing and operationalizes the transposition of Minsky's financial postures to the peripheral central bank's own balance sheet \citep{Foley2003,ChamorroFutinico2025} (Section~\ref{sec:B5_foley_chamorro_minsky}).

\textbf{A.5} Taking continuous growth rates of Eq.~\eqref{app:eq:solvr_def} yields the continuous growth decomposition:
\begin{equation}
	\label{app:eq:solvr_growth}
	g_{\text{SolvR},t} \;=\; g_{F,t} \;-\; g_{M,t} \;=\; g_{e,t} \;+\; g_{IR,t} \;-\; g_{M,t}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{app:eq:solvr_growth} isolates three distinct structural channels driving peripheral balance-sheet solvency:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
	\item \textbf{External Balance-of-Payments Channel} ($g_{IR,t}$, expanding solvency): Export revenues and net foreign capital inflows accumulate foreign exchange reserves, bolstering central bank solvency. Current account deficits, foreign debt service, and transnational profit repatriation drain reserves, pulling $\text{SolvR}_t$ downward.
	\item \textbf{Valuation Channel} ($g_{e,t}$, expanding nominal solvency): Nominal currency depreciation ($e_t \uparrow$) increases the domestic-currency accounting valuation of foreign assets ($F_t = e_t \cdot IR_t$). While this mechanically cushions $\text{SolvR}_t$ in domestic currency units, it carries acute real macroeconomic costs by inflating imported intermediate input costs and triggering domestic wage-price defense spirals.
	\item \textbf{Domestic Dilution Channel} ($g_{M,t}$, compressing solvency): Broad money creation, fiscal monetization, and emergency Lender-of-Last-Resort liquidity expand domestic obligations from below, continuously diluting the world-money backing of the monetary base.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Current and Capital Account Decomposition}
\label{app:current_account}

\textbf{A.6} The evolution of the central bank's foreign-currency asset base is governed by the external balance of payments. In domestic currency terms, the current account ($CA_t$) and capital account ($KA_t$) are specified as:
\begin{equation}
	\label{app:eq:CA}
	CA_t \;=\; e_t \cdot P_{Q,t}^{USD}\!\left(\text{ToT}_t \cdot X_t - Q_t\right) \;-\; e_t \cdot r_t \cdot D_t^{ext} \;+\; T_t
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
	\label{app:eq:KA}
	KA_t \;=\; e_t \cdot \Delta D_t^{ext} \;+\; e_t \cdot FDI_t \;-\; e_t \cdot \Pi_t
\end{equation}
The sum of the current and capital accounts determines the net balance-of-payments flow ($\text{BoP}_t$), which dictates the net change in world-money holdings:
\begin{equation}
	\label{app:eq:bop}
	\Delta F_t \;\equiv\; \Delta(e_t \cdot IR_t) \;=\; CA_t \;+\; KA_t \;\equiv\; \text{BoP}_t
\end{equation}

\subsection{Derivation of the Discrete Solvency Law of Motion}
\label{app:solvr_substitution}

\textbf{A.7} From Eq.~\eqref{app:eq:solvr_def}, the total stock of world-money assets held by the central bank decomposes into the product of the Solvency Ratio and domestic monetary obligations:
\begin{equation}
	\label{app:eq:solvr_sub}
	F_t \;=\; \text{SolvR}_t \cdot M_t
\end{equation}
Applying the exact discrete product rule $\Delta(A_t B_t) = B_t \Delta A_t + A_{t-1} \Delta B_t$ with $A_t = \text{SolvR}_t$ and $B_t = M_t$ yields:
\begin{equation}
	\label{app:eq:solvr_expand}
	\Delta F_t \;=\; M_t \cdot \Delta\text{SolvR}_t \;+\; \text{SolvR}_{t-1} \cdot \Delta M_t
\end{equation}
Dividing Eq.~\eqref{app:eq:solvr_expand} through by $M_t$ and substituting $\Delta F_t = \text{BoP}_t$ from Eq.~\eqref{app:eq:bop}:
\begin{equation}
	\frac{\text{BoP}_t}{M_t} \;=\; \Delta\text{SolvR}_t \;+\; \text{SolvR}_{t-1} \cdot \frac{\Delta M_t}{M_t}
\end{equation}
Rearranging terms isolates the exact discrete law of motion for the Solvency Ratio:
\begin{equation}
	\label{app:eq:solvr_lom}
	\boxed{\;\Delta\text{SolvR}_t \;=\; \frac{\text{BoP}_t}{M_t} \;-\; \text{SolvR}_{t-1} \cdot g_{M,t}\;}
\end{equation}
where $g_{M,t} \equiv \Delta M_t / M_t$ is the simple growth rate of domestic monetary liabilities.

\textbf{A.8} Equation~\eqref{app:eq:solvr_lom} delivers an intuitive and powerful economic decomposition:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em]
	\item The first term, $\text{BoP}_t / M_t$, represents the \textbf{external liquidity injection rate}---net foreign exchange inflows scaled directly by the existing domestic credit pyramid. Balance-of-payments surpluses expand the asset base and bolster solvency.
	\item The second term, $\text{SolvR}_{t-1} \cdot g_{M,t}$, represents the \textbf{domestic dilution drag}. Rapid domestic money growth ($g_{M,t} > 0$) dilutes the inherited backing ratio. The presence of the lagged state variable $\text{SolvR}_{t-1}$ is an exact algebraic consequence of the discrete product rule: domestic monetary expansion dilutes the preexisting stock of world-money cover.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{A.9} For completeness, the dual discrete law of motion for the leverage ratio ($\text{LevR}_t \equiv 1/\text{SolvR}_t$) follows symmetrically from $M_t = \text{LevR}_t \cdot F_t$:
\begin{equation}
	\label{app:eq:levr_lom}
	\Delta\text{LevR}_t \;=\; \frac{\Delta M_t}{e_t \cdot IR_t} \;-\; \text{LevR}_{t-1} \cdot g_{F,t}
\end{equation}
Both laws express the identical balance-sheet reality: in solvency form (Eq.~\ref{app:eq:solvr_lom}), foreign inflows drive recovery while domestic monetization acts as a dilution drag; in leverage form (Eq.~\ref{app:eq:levr_lom}), domestic money creation drives leverage expansion while reserve accumulation acts as an absorption multiplier.

\subsection{Analytical Convexity and Non-Linear Reserve Exhaustion}
\label{app:convexity}

\textbf{A.10} The structural vulnerability of peripheral central banks is amplified by the non-linear curvature of balance-sheet solvency. Taking the partial derivatives of $\text{SolvR}_t$ with respect to domestic obligations $M_t$ and foreign assets $F_t$:
\begin{equation}
	\label{app:eq:solvr_derivatives}
	\frac{\partial \text{SolvR}_t}{\partial M_t} \;=\; -\frac{F_t}{M_t^2} \;<\; 0, \qquad \frac{\partial^2 \text{SolvR}_t}{\partial M_t^2} \;=\; \frac{2 F_t}{M_t^3} \;>\; 0 \quad (\forall M_t > 0, F_t > 0)
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
	\frac{\partial \text{SolvR}_t}{\partial F_t} \;=\; \frac{1}{M_t} \;>\; 0, \qquad \frac{\partial^2 \text{SolvR}_t}{\partial F_t^2} \;=\; 0
\end{equation}
As gross international reserves approach exhaustion ($IR_t \to 0 \implies F_t \to 0$), the absolute buffer $\text{SolvR}_t$ approaches zero. In this depleted state, any incremental withdrawal of foreign currency or external import shock triggers an acute percentage collapse in central bank credibility, permanently crippling the authority's capacity to anchor expectations.

\subsection{Constraint-Binding Conditions and Structural Minskian Regimes}
\label{app:constraint_cases}

\textbf{A.11} The central bank's solvency position deteriorates ($\Delta\text{SolvR}_t < 0$) whenever domestic monetary dilution outpaces net balance-of-payments inflows:
\begin{equation}
	\label{app:eq:binding}
	\Delta\text{SolvR}_t \;<\; 0 \;\iff\; \frac{\text{BoP}_t}{M_t} \;<\; \text{SolvR}_{t-1} \cdot g_{M,t} \;\iff\; g_{M,t} \;>\; \frac{\text{BoP}_t}{F_{t-1}}
\end{equation}
Even during an external commodity price boom ($\text{BoP}_t > 0$, such as the 1971--1972 copper price high), central bank solvency deteriorates if domestic credit expansion outpaces the growth rate of foreign reserves.

\textbf{A.12} In my structural Post-Keynesian framework, monetization is an endogenous institutional response to distributive conflict and foreign-exchange bottlenecks rather than an exogenous policy choice. The state-dependent parameter $\beta_{gM}(\text{SolvR}_{t-1})$ characterizes the regime-conditional absorption capacity of the peripheral monetary system. Following the 3-regime Threshold Vector Autoregression estimated in Section~\ref{sec:empirical_tournament}, the macro-system transitions across three discrete Minskian states:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
	\item \textbf{Hedge Regime ($\text{SolvR}_{t-1} > \hat{\gamma}_H = 0.21$):} Reserve backing comfortably validates domestic liquid obligations. The central bank credibly maintains currency stability, and domestic monetary expansion is absorbed without price instability ($\beta_{\text{Hedge}} \approx 0.00\%$).
	\item \textbf{Speculative Regime ($0.14 < \text{SolvR}_{t-1} \le 0.21$):} Reserve buffers are partially depleted. Domestic credit growth begins to strain central bank credibility, and the pass-through of money to prices increases to $+0.54\%$.
	\item \textbf{Forced Ponzi Regime ($\text{SolvR}_{t-1} \le \hat{\gamma}_P = 0.14$):} Foreign exchange reserves are exhausted. The central bank is forced into emergency Lender-of-Last-Resort monetization to avert systemic enterprise insolvencies. Because world-money backing is gone, emergency liquidity cannot be absorbed by the balance sheet and passes directly into hyperinflation and currency flight ($\beta_{\text{Ponzi}} = +4.54\%$).
\end{enumerate}
This threshold formalization establishes that hyperinflation is a non-linear property of balance-sheet solvency exhaustion under external financial subordination, not a linear artifact of money creation.

## Section: appendix/appendix_causal_pcmci.tex

\section{Non-Parametric Causal Discovery (Runge et al., 2019) as Structural Robustness for the Monthly Threshold VAR Architecture}
\label{app:causal_pcmci}

\textbf{F.1} To establish causal directionality without imposing restrictive linear structural identification restrictions, this appendix presents the non-parametric causal discovery analysis using the Peter-Clark Momentary Conditional Independence (PCMCI) framework developed by \citet{Runge2019}. Standard time-series identification confronts three methodological pathologies: (1) \emph{bivariate naivety}, where pairwise tests mistake indirect paths and common drivers for direct causation; (2) \emph{the FullCI trap}, where saturated conditioning on the entire past of all variables destroys degrees of freedom and induces collider bias; and (3) \emph{autocorrelation memory}, where internal persistence inflates false-positive causal arrows. In peripheral open economies, the external trade account operates across two distinct domains: the real purchasing power blade ($M^{\text{cap}}_t$, Prebisch-ECLAC capacity to import) and the real structural imbalance blade ($\Theta_t \equiv M_t / M^{\text{cap}}_t \times 100$). Because imperial credit embargoes enforced hard foreign-currency quantity rationing, the real structural imbalance diverged from domestic nominal relative prices. PCMCI provides the non-parametric causal discovery foundation that reconciles both blades.

\subsection{The PCMCI Algorithmic Protocol}
\label{app:pcmci_protocol}

\textbf{F.2} PCMCI addresses high-dimensional, autocorrelated time series through a two-step conditioning pipeline:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
	\item \textbf{Step 1 (PC1 Conditioning Selection):} For each variable $X^j_t$, the algorithm applies an iterative conditional independence search to identify the minimal set of relevant parents $\hat{\mathcal{P}}(X^j_t)$ across time lags $\tau \in \{1, 2, \dots, \tau_{\max}\}$, discarding conditionally uninformative predictors.
	\item \textbf{Step 2 (Momentary Conditional Independence Test, MCI):} For candidate link $X^i_{t-\tau} \to X^j_t$, the algorithm tests conditional independence:
	\begin{equation}
		\text{MCI}: \quad X^i_{t-\tau} \;\perp\!\!\!\perp\; X^j_t \;\big|\; \hat{\mathcal{P}}(X^j_t) \setminus \{X^i_{t-\tau}\}, \; \hat{\mathcal{P}}(X^i_{t-\tau})
		\label{eq:mci_test}
	\end{equation}
	conditioning simultaneously on the parents of the target and the parents of the driver. This conditions out both autocorrelation within each series and contemporaneous common-driver confounding.
\end{enumerate}

	\item \textbf{Validation of the Solvency Threshold Gate:} Selecting $\text{SolvR}$ as the regime threshold variable $I\{\gamma_{k-1} < \text{SolvR}_{t-1} \le \gamma_k\}$ is independently validated by the discovered graph: $\Delta \text{SolvR}_{t-3} \to \pi_t$ is confirmed as a direct structural link ($\text{Partial Corr} = -0.128, p = 0.0497$), identifying Central Bank solvency as the primary structural gatekeeper of domestic price formation.

PCMCI reveals that the partial correlations running from base money growth to price inflation ($+0.114, p = 0.0834$, no direct link at $\alpha=0.05$), external structural imbalance ($-0.055, p = 0.3959$), and Central Bank solvency ($+0.085, p = 0.1949$) are statistically indistinguishable from zero, decisively refuting the Monetarist DAG.
	\item \textbf{Autonomous Enclave Exogeneity of Mining Extraction:} In an expanded 8-variable system incorporating physical mining extraction ($g_{\text{Mining},m} \equiv \Delta \ln Q_{\text{Mining},m}$), PCMCI verifies that copper extraction functioned as an autonomous primary export enclave. \subsection{Productive Capital Stock, Profit Rate, and Net Accumulation Framework (1960--1975)}
\label{app:profit_accumulation_ledger}

\textbf{G.6} This subsection formalizes the balance-sheet accounting, Generalized Perpetual Inventory Method (GPIM) depreciation formulas, and Weisskopf-Marxian decomposition equations supporting Section~4 §4.10 regarding the Marxian profit squeeze and net capital accumulation.

\paragraph{Productive Fixed Capital Stock Harmonization.}
Following the capital stock harmonization methodology established in Chapter~2, the capital stock denominator is restricted strictly to productive fixed assets, encompassing Machinery and Equipment ($ME$) and Non-Residential Construction ($NRC$):
\begin{equation}
	K_t \equiv K_{\text{ME}, t} + K_{\text{NRC}, t}
	\label{eq:k_definition_app}
\end{equation}
Residential Construction ($RC$) is explicitly excluded because housing represents social overhead infrastructure and durable consumption yielding utility rather than direct surplus-value producing capital.

\paragraph{The Weisskopf Decomposition.}
Drawing on the Classical-Marxian and Post-Keynesian traditions \citep{Weisskopf1979,Foley1982,Basu2022}, the aggregate profit rate on productive capital ($r_t$) at current replacement cost decomposes into the profit share, capacity utilization, and potential capital productivity:
\begin{equation}
	r_t \equiv \frac{\Pi_t}{P_{K, t} K_t} = (1 - \omega_t) \cdot \frac{Y_t}{P_{K, t} K_t} = (1 - \omega_t) \cdot \mu_t \cdot \sigma_t
	\label{eq:profit_rate_weisskopf_app}
\end{equation}
where $\Pi_t \equiv (1 - \omega_t) Y^{\text{nom}}_t$ is the nominal mass of aggregate profits (surplus value), $\omega_t$ is Astorga's \citeyearpar{Astorga2023} aggregate wage share, $1 - \omega_t$ is the profit share, $\mu_t \equiv Y_t / Y^p_t$ is structural capacity utilization (estimated via Cointegrating Multivariate Polynomial Regression IM-OLS in Chapter~2), $\sigma_t \equiv Y^p_t / (P_{K, t} K_t)$ is potential capital productivity, and $Y_t / (P_{K, t} K_t) = \mu_t \sigma_t$ is the actual output-capital ratio at current replacement cost. In standard macroeconomic notation, I denote the aggregate profit rate by $r$, reserving $u$ for the open unemployment rate, $\mu$ for capacity utilization, and $i$ for nominal interest rates.

\paragraph{Implicit Physical Depreciation and Net Accumulation.}
Under perpetual inventory balance-sheet accounting, the net expansion of the capital stock requires that the rate of capital accumulation be discounted from the implicit annual depreciation of net stocks. Implicit annual physical depreciation ($D_t$) and the implicit physical depreciation rate ($\delta_t$) are defined as:
\begin{equation}
	D_t \equiv K_{t-1} + I_t - K_t \qquad \text{and} \qquad \delta_t \equiv \frac{D_t}{K_{t-1}}
	\label{eq:implicit_depreciation_app}
\end{equation}
where $I_t \equiv I_{\text{ME}, t} + I_{\text{NRC}, t}$ is real gross fixed investment in productive assets. 

The Net Rate of Capital Accumulation ($g^n_{K, t}$) and the Re-capitalization Propensity out of surplus value ($\chi_t \equiv I^{\text{net}}_t / \Pi_t$) are governed by:
\begin{equation}
	g^n_{K, t} \equiv \frac{I_t - D_t}{K_{t-1}} = g^{\text{gross}}_{K, t} - \delta_t = \chi_t \cdot r_t = \chi_t \cdot (1 - \omega_t) \cdot \mu_t \cdot \sigma_t
	\label{eq:net_accumulation_rate_app}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:net_accumulation_rate_app} formalizes the Cambridge-Marxian accumulation identity: the net growth rate of productive capital equals the product of the re-capitalization propensity ($\chi$) and the aggregate profit rate ($r$).
