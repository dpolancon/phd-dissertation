The Unidad Popular's electoral victory in 1970 brought a project of socialist transformation into contact with popular organizations already contesting authority over work, land, and urban life. Industrial workers, the peasantry, and *pobladores* did not enter politics through the same institutions or pursue identical demands. Their struggles nevertheless became increasingly connected before the election. This subsection follows successive waves of mobilization, repression, and political articulation to explain that connection. Chile's changing economy supplied the structural setting, but did not dictate the political outcome.

During the nitrate era, export receipts sustained public revenues and domestic activity while tying employment, imports, and fiscal capacity to external demand and finance. The postwar dislocation of natural nitrate weakened that arrangement before the Great Depression. The Depression intensified an existing crisis of the export economy and accelerated protection of domestic production and state intervention. Manufacturing development and the institutional conditions for industrialization thus preceded 1929; the crisis changed their scale and direction rather than initiating them from nothing (Pinto1958,Palma1984,Silva2007).

After the Second World War, mass production, productivity growth, rising wages, collective bargaining, and mass consumption became linked in the North Atlantic core (Vidal2019). US-centered monetary and political leadership reorganized international hierarchy without removing the unequal conditions under which peripheral economies industrialized. Chile expanded production for its domestic market while continuing to require imported machinery, intermediate inputs, technology, and foreign exchange. Industrial growth could therefore reproduce external dependence in a new form (DeVylder1976,Silva2007).

Industrialization also changed the spatial distribution of labor. Figure [fig:sec51_urban_labor] places declining primary-sector shares beside rising urbanization and tertiary activity. The secondary sector did not become the principal absorber of the expanding labor force as Chile urbanized. This relative shortfall supports the characterization of *disarticulated industrialization*: industrial expansion coexisted with a wider transformation of settlement and livelihoods (DeVylder1976). The conflicts that developed on this terrain, rather than the sectoral series themselves, establish the chronology that follows.

## Wave I: Selective incorporation and coercive backlash.

The first conflict wave began before the profitability series available here starts in 1943. Popular Front politics brought parties and organized constituencies into a common electoral project, while access to collective representation remained uneven across workplaces and the countryside (Milos2008,Loveman1976). Rural workers made claims over employment, labor conditions, and the right to organize; the countryside was not merely a passive source of labor for urban industry. The 1943--47 accounting interval captures an observed economic segment of this longer confrontation, not its historical origin.

By the mid-1940s, expanding rural demands formed part of a wider confrontation over labor organization and the terms of work. The Plaza Bulnes massacre and the national stoppages of January and February 1946 exposed its urban and industrial dimensions (BravoVargas2017; pp. 199--201, 213--225). The agricultural petitions assembled by Loveman document the rural dimension and its crest in 1947 (Loveman1976). Their distributive significance lay partly in workers' capacity to turn shared grievances into demands extending beyond an individual workplace. Over the observed 1943--47 interval, a declining profit share was the principal negative contribution to profit-rate growth in the chapter's accounting (Table [tab:profitability_decomposition]).

The response in the countryside combined formal recognition with restrictions on the scale of organization. Law 8,811, published in July 1947, recognized agricultural unions while confining them to individual *fundos* and prohibiting combinations of those unions. Eligibility requirements tied organization to a sufficiently large and stable workforce on each property; restrictions on petition filing during sowing and harvest and the resort to binding arbitration further narrowed collective action (Chile1947; arts. 9, 14, 16, 53, 60--69). The law made the boundaries of landed estates the boundaries of legal representation, obstructing the organization of common agrarian demands across them. Recognition carried material benefits and limited protections, but preserved constraints on the pursuit of collective claims.

Law 8,987, published in September 1948, extended restriction through political exclusion. Alongside outlawing the Communist Party, it connected exclusion from the electoral registers to exclusion from union membership and barred affected workers from union leadership, labor delegations, and conciliation bodies, including agricultural arbitration boards (Chile1948; art. 1, amendment 5; art. 7, amendment 1; art. 8). The two laws restricted different levels of organization: combination across estates and political representation within a broader national confrontation. The subsequent decline in registered agricultural petitions marks the contraction of these channels, not the disappearance of agrarian grievances.

## Wave II: Coercive containment and urban recomposition.

The restrictions imposed in 1947--48 narrowed the institutional channels through which workers could press their demands. In the countryside, legal recognition bound union organization to the estate; across sectors, anticommunist exclusion attacked the political networks through which collective claims acquired a wider reach. Conflict continued under conditions increasingly unfavorable to workers' efforts to defend wages and influence economic policy (Thielemann2023; pp. 47--53).

Stabilization brought this conflict over distribution into sharper focus. Under Ib\'a\ nez, the US advisory firm Klein--Saks supplied a technical rationale for policies restraining wage adjustment in the name of controlling inflation. As (Thielemann2023; pp. 45--53) argues, these measures consolidated a deterioration in real wages already underway rather than initiating it. Limits on wage adjustment, unemployment, and repression of organized resistance shifted adjustment costs onto working-class households, while rising prices for essential goods and services extended the dispute beyond the workplace.

The profitability accounts place this distributive conflict alongside a continuing transformation of production. Over 1948--58, the rising profit share supplied the largest positive contribution to profit-rate growth. Capital deepening more than offset the positive contribution of potential labor productivity: their annualized contributions were $-2.60$ and $+2.29$ log points, respectively (Table [tab:profitability_decomposition]). Capital per worker expanded faster than potential output per worker, while distribution supported profitability. This greater capital intensity coexisted with the limited relative absorption of the growing labor force described above.

Urban growth made housing central to the conflict over living standards. A place in the city required more than access to wage employment: households confronted scarce housing, high rents, inadequate services, and the cost of reaching work. These pressures cut across the division between securely employed workers and residents with irregular incomes. Thielemann traces how disputes over transport fares, rents, and basic consumption connected grievances at work with organization in neighborhoods. The city became a field of collective action in its own right (Thielemann2023; pp. 51--53).

The revolt of April 1957 and the occupation that founded La Victoria in October gave this urban conflict a new political expression. They were distinct forms of action, but Thielemann places them within a shared process through which popular groups challenged the limits of institutional representation and expanded their capacity to act collectively. La Victoria made occupation and neighborhood construction into a practical claim to housing. The significance of 1957 lies in this political awakening rather than an immediate reversal of labor's economic position. It opened a phase of struggle whose organizations, alliances, and objectives developed unevenly through the Long Sixties (Thielemann2023).

## Wave III: The Long Sixties and the rise of the Unidad Popular.

Industrial workers entered the Long Sixties with demands reaching beyond wage adjustment to the organization of production. Disputes over rationalization and work intensity challenged managerial authority, while solidarity linked workers across industries: Huachipato steelworkers supported the Lota miners in the long strike of 1960. Repression shaped collective memory, including the killings at El Salvador in 1966. These experiences helped radicalize sections of the movement before the UP took office (Thielemann2018; pp. 119--125).

In the countryside, agrarian reform placed landownership and the organization of rural labor within the same political struggle (Loveman1976). The 1967 union law changed the territorial basis on which workers could act. Membership could extend across estates, the minimum territorial unit became the commune, and unions could form federations and confederations (Chile1967; arts. 1, 3--4). Compared with the estate-bound framework of 1947, this widened the institutional scope for collective demands. The agrarian question concerned both access to land and the power to organize across the boundaries of individual properties.

In the cities, *pobladores* pressed claims to land, housing, and public services through neighborhood organization and occupations. Their mobilization challenged who could secure a place in the city and on what terms. Housing was both a material need and a demand for political recognition, bringing everyday life into conflicts over property and public provision (Garces2002,Murphy2015). These struggles had their own organizations and objectives, even where their participants also experienced conflict as wage earners.

Concepci\'on illustrates the local relationships through which political connections developed. Schlotterbeck locates organizing in the university, nearby industrial communities, and working-class neighborhoods, where labor and student activism intersected with struggles over housing. These relationships preceded Allende's victory, while their development under the UP opened further possibilities for coordinated action (Schlotterbeck2018; pp. 7, 11--14). Popular articulation was a process, not a unity completed at the ballot box.

Widening mobilization did not coincide with a continuous deterioration of aggregate profitability. Across the 1959--70 accounting interval, the profit rate rose, supported by positive contributions from the profit share, utilization, and potential labor productivity, with capital deepening providing an offset (Table [tab:profitability_decomposition]). The conflict concerned the distribution and control of an expanding economy as well as its unresolved limits. By 1970, struggles over work, land, and housing confronted the question of executive power; their convergence made a common political project possible without erasing differences over its objectives or means.

## Wave IV: The UP project and the crisis of accumulation.

Allende's election in 1970 brought these accumulated struggles into a new relationship with executive power. The UP's project connected redistribution to changes in ownership and the direction of production. In Arboleda's account, Pedro Vuskovic and Jacques Chonchol approached planning as part of national liberation: transforming the economy required both public intervention and greater popular power (Arboleda2023). The government encountered organizations with their own demands and rhythms of action. Winn's distinction between revolution from above and below captures this relationship between a state project and popular initiatives that could support it while pressing beyond its immediate limits (Winn1986,Winn2016).

Political articulation continued after the election. In Concepci\'on, the People's Assembly of 1972 brought together sectors whose organizing Schlotterbeck traces through the preceding years (Schlotterbeck2018; p. 14). This local convergence illustrates why the rise of the UP cannot be reduced to its electoral coalition: participation also developed through institutions and practices created within the movements themselves.

These transformations unfolded alongside a sharp deterioration in profitability and productive accumulation. In the chapter's accounts, the profit rate fell from 12.23 percent in 1970 to 6.57 percent in 1972, while net accumulation slowed from 4.23 to 1.85 percent. Gross accumulation fell from 11.63 to 7.64 percent over the same years (Table [tab:accumulation_accounting]). The annual observations for 1973 combine a profit-rate rebound with a further slowing of net accumulation to 1.71 percent; they span both the final months of the UP and the first months after the coup. This sequence directs attention from profitability alone to the conditions under which surplus was converted into productive investment.

The confrontation over property was also visible in financial markets: share prices fell sharply after Allende's election and rose after the military coup (GirardiBowles2018). Productive accumulation, however, remained weak in the annual accounts. The coup of September 1973 ended the UP experiment through force, not through the automatic resolution of an economic adjustment.
