\section*{The Problem of Productive Capacity and Capitalist Reproduction}

Actual output is observed; productive capacity is not directly observed. Capacity utilization, $\mu \equiv Y/Y^p$, relates realized output $Y$ to a productive ceiling $Y^p$ whose identification requires an account of what the accumulated capital stock can produce. Surveys, engineering benchmarks, and statistical estimates supply different answers to that question. Treating their denominator as a neutral technical datum leaves unexplained the economic and institutional relations through which investment becomes usable capacity.

This dissertation investigates capitalist reproduction when accumulation, productive capacity, and macroeconomic stability do not move together automatically. The three essays move from the identification of capacity, through the structural determinants of its formation, to the historical operation of external and monetary constraints during Chile's Unidad Popular. They find, respectively, that an autonomous output--capital benchmark does not survive the full-sample system tests, that the transformation of accumulation into capacity is non-unitary and distributionally conditioned, and that monetary transmission in the Chilean case depends on external solvency conditions. Their common concern is how production can be reproduced under distributive conflict and institutional and international constraints; each addresses a different part of that problem.

The starting point is the distinction between capital accumulation and capacity formation. If productive capacity is assumed to expand proportionally with capital, the long-run capacity-to-capital ratio is fixed before it is measured. Chapters~1 and~2 instead investigate the transformation elasticity, $\theta \equiv \partial\ln Y^p/\partial\ln K$, where $K$ denotes the capital stock. The benchmark $\theta=1$ expresses proportional expansion. An elasticity below unity means that, within the specified relation, capacity grows less than proportionally with capital. The essays call this tendency structural overaccumulation. That definition does not by itself establish a complete theory of crisis.

Opening the transformation relation brings the balanced- and unbalanced-growth problem into the measurement of utilization. Decentralized investment decisions need not generate proportional reproduction, while the choice of technique depends on distribution and on how firms organize production. Machinery, structures, and intellectual property also enter the capital stock in different proportions. These are distinct dimensions: functional income distribution concerns wages and profits; capital composition concerns the assets accumulated; labor-process conflict concerns the organization of control and cooperation inside production. Productive capacity is formed through these relations. Utilization measures actual output relative to the resulting capacity.

For an import-dependent economy, the transformation also requires access to foreign exchange and external finance. A firm can retain an incentive to mechanize while facing limits on its ability to import machinery. During a peripheral crisis, however, the problem extends beyond long-run capacity formation to the reproduction of payments, enterprise liquidity, and international settlement. These questions require a historical account of institutions and agency. They provide the connection to Chapter~3 without making its monetary analysis another estimate of $\theta$.

\section*{Fields of Inquiry and Research Questions}

The dissertation enters four connected fields. The first is \emph{capacity utilization, accumulation, and heterodox macroeconomics}: Post-Keynesian and Kaleckian debates over normal and realized utilization, Sraffian approaches to distribution and normal capital productivity, and classical-Marxian accounts of accumulation and profitability. As the first two essays discuss, a disagreement over the adjustment of utilization also raises a prior question about the denominator being adjusted \citep{Kurz1986,Shaikh2016}. The dissertation brings that identification problem into the debate over balanced and unbalanced growth.

The second field is \emph{technical change, choice of technique, and the political economy of the firm}. Chapter~2 connects decentralized accumulation to the internally divided organization that converts investment into operating capacity, drawing on the frameworks of \citet{Okishio1961,Okishio2022} and \citet{Vidal2019,Vidal2022}. Mechanization responds to distributive pressure, but its capacity consequences depend on capital composition and the organization of production. This field supplies the structural mechanism left open by the first essay's measurement results.

The third is \emph{structuralist and dependency political economy}. Import dependence, uneven development, and the balance-of-payments constraint enter the explanation of why the conditions of accumulation differ between center and periphery. Chapter~2 asks how foreign-exchange availability conditions the choice of technique; Chapter~3 investigates the broader relations of financial subordination and international settlement. A balance-of-payments constraint limits import financing, whereas financial subordination describes an asymmetric international relation. The latter helps explain the conditions under which the former operates, but the two are not interchangeable.

The fourth is \emph{historical and international monetary political economy}, centered on the Unidad Popular. Chapter~3 compares competing interpretations of the Chilean crisis and develops dependency theory as a research programme \citep{Lakatos1978,Kvangraven2021}. World money, international currency hierarchy, central-bank balance sheets, and endogenous monetary accommodation enter an inquiry that also requires historical contingency and institutional agency. History of economic thought is a transversal methodological dimension: Chapter~1 examines the premises embedded in capacity measurement, while Chapter~3 asks how competing research programmes select and interpret the historical evidence.

Three questions organize this inquiry:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.8em]
  \item \textbf{Measurement and identification.} How can productive capacity be identified empirically when it is unobserved and the long-run output--capital relation is historically and distributionally conditioned?
  \item \textbf{Accumulation and structural mechanism.} What determines how accumulated capital becomes productive capacity, and how do distribution, choice of technique, capital composition, and external constraints condition that transformation?
  \item \textbf{Peripheral crisis and historical political economy.} How do external finance, foreign-exchange solvency, distributive conflict, international monetary conditions, and institutional agency shape macroeconomic transmission during a peripheral structural transformation such as the Unidad Popular?
\end{enumerate}

The United States and Chile belong together through an analytically asymmetric comparison. The US evidence provides a setting for examining capacity measurement and long-run relations among accumulation, distribution, technique, and capital composition within a center economy. Chile introduces conditions that cannot simply be inferred from that setting: dependence on imported capital goods, limits on foreign-exchange availability, financial subordination, and peripheral monetary institutions. Center--periphery therefore changes the analytical problem rather than merely supplying a second country observation. The comparison does not establish a universal two-country law. In particular, Chapter~2 uses different specifications for the two economies, so its comparative findings concern mechanisms and the direction of relationships rather than directly interchangeable coefficient estimates.

\section*{From Measurement to Mechanism to Historical Crisis}

Chapter~1 asks whether the long-run output--capital relation can sustain an autonomous empirical benchmark for productive capacity. Its critical replication identifies the distributional and historical conditioning of the relation that survives system testing. This raises the question subsequently examined in Chapter~2: how does distribution enter the conversion of accumulated capital into capacity? The second essay develops the dissertation's principal structural account and extends it to the external constraint facing peripheral accumulation.

Chapter~3 examines the historically specific operation of external and monetary constraints during the Unidad Popular. The question now concerns crisis transmission and institutional response, including how international money and the central bank's balance sheet condition domestic reproduction. The chapter inherits the broader problem of external constraint from Chapter~2, but it introduces a different object of explanation and a different evidentiary design. Its historical argument and dynamic estimates do not statistically validate the long-run transformation model.

\section*{Contributions of the Dissertation}

\subsection*{Capacity Measurement and Heterodox Macroeconomics}

The dissertation treats the productive-capacity denominator as an object requiring identification. Within Chapter~1's full-sample US replication, output and capital alone do not sustain the required long-run system properties. A relation survives when the rate of exploitation and historical breaks enter the system. The contribution is to challenge the interpretation of the output--capital coefficient as an insulated technical constant. It also qualifies the use of a fixed denominator in debates about normal utilization: the conditions of capacity formation must be examined before deviations from capacity can be interpreted.

\subsection*{Accumulation, Technical Change, and Capacity Formation}

Chapter~2 turns the non-unitary output--capital result into a structural question and an estimable relation. Its account links distribution and cost-minimizing technique to decentralized investment and conflict within the firm, then incorporates heterogeneous capital composition. Accumulation can therefore expand without generating a proportional increase in productive capacity. This contribution connects the political economy of production to aggregate capacity measurement. The estimates characterize the resulting macroeconomic relation; they do not separately identify every workplace mechanism invoked by the theory.

\subsection*{Center--Periphery Political Economy}

The Chilean extension shows why the external constraint changes the feasible transformation process when it binds. An incentive to substitute machinery for labor is insufficient if the means of acquiring imported equipment are restricted. In the estimated constrained state, the mechanization response is sharply attenuated. Foreign exchange thus enters the choice-of-technique mechanism itself, rather than serving only as another regression control. Read alongside Chapter~3, this result connects the material conditions of investment to the monetary and financial relations through which peripheral reproduction is sustained or disrupted.

\subsection*{Historical Political Economy, Dependency, and International Money}

Chapter~3 reopens the Unidad Popular debate by linking a comparison of research programmes to historical reconstruction and an open-economy central-bank balance-sheet framework. It formalizes peripheral solvency in terms of foreign reserve assets relative to domestic monetary liabilities, while distinguishing international structural constraints from institutional choices. Its dynamic evidence is consistent with endogenous monetary accommodation under adverse external conditions and challenges accounts that treat money creation as an autonomous explanation of Chilean inflation \citep{DornbuschEdwards1990,Edwards2024}. The contribution is an empirically grounded alternative interpretation whose causal reach remains bounded by the historical and time-series evidence.

\subsection*{Data, Measurement, and Empirical Strategy}

These intellectual contributions rest on reconstructed historical capital stocks, archival monthly monetary and external series, staged critical replication, long-run cointegrating estimation, and threshold estimation of state-dependent relations. The data and methods make different aspects of reproduction observable and contestable. Their value lies in the questions they permit the essays to answer, rather than in a common estimator applied across all three.

\section*{Research Design, Evidence, and Levels of Analysis}

Chapter~1 uses critical replication and system testing to answer an identification question. A single-equation fit can recover a plausible capacity path without establishing that the underlying variables sustain a stable long-run relation. The chapter therefore examines whether the benchmark survives changes in specification and joint-system tests. Chapter~2 combines theoretical construction with long-run comparative estimation to investigate the mechanism connecting accumulation to capacity. Historical shifts and, in Chile, an estimated external-constraint threshold enter that design.

Chapter~3 combines historical reconstruction, archival evidence, directional-precedence testing, and non-linear dynamics to investigate historically specific crisis transmission. Its monthly estimates use a broader historical sample to interpret the Unidad Popular episode; they are not estimates based only on the government's three years. They identify systematic dynamic patterns and state-dependent conditional responses. Unobserved common shocks and structural breaks remain limitations, and the analysis does not provide experimental micro-level counterfactual causal identification.

Methodological plurality does not imply that evidence in one essay statistically validates the model or findings of another. Nor do the different estimated utilization paths or transformation elasticities constitute a single harmonized series. The evidentiary division of labor supports a common argument about reproduction while preserving the identification conditions and limits of each essay.

\section*{The Three Essays}

\subsection*{Essay 1: Measurement and Identification}

\emph{Critical Replication of Shaikh's Capacity Utilization Measure} asks whether an accounting-based output--capital relation can supply a capacity benchmark independent of distribution. It enters the heterodox measurement debate through a critical replication of \citet{Shaikh2016} for the US corporate sector, 1947--2011. The design reconstructs the baseline, opens the single-equation specification space, and tests the relation at system level.

The full-sample bivariate system does not sustain cointegration and dynamic stability. The retained trivariate specifications include the rate of exploitation, defined as the ratio of profit share to wage share, together with historical controls; their transformation elasticity is below unity. The finding makes distributional conditioning part of the identification problem. It does not establish that this measured distributive ratio directly captures all labor-process conflict. The aggregate, single-sector framework and time-invariant parameterization limit the interpretation, and the mechanism of capacity formation remains the question for the next essay.

\subsection*{Essay 2: Structural Mechanism and Comparative Extension}

\emph{The Transformation of Accumulation into Productive Capacities: Capacity Utilization in the Center and Periphery} asks what determines the conversion of accumulation into capacity. It connects classical choice of technique to the political economy of the firm and then to the external constraint. The theoretical analysis and long-run estimation cover the United States over 1931--2024 and Chile over 1943--2010.

For the United States, the estimated elasticity remains below unity and varies with effective wage pressure and capital composition. Intellectual property enters as a component of capital, not as a functional income share. For Chile, the wage-share elasticity of the capital composition gap falls from approximately $3.29$ in the unconstrained state to $0.14$ in the constrained state. This is evidence of a sharply attenuated mechanization response, not an assertion that all investment ceases. The chapter supplies the principal structural mechanism of the dissertation while explicitly stopping short of a complete crisis theory, a fully specified profitability theory, or an exhaustive dependency framework.

\subsection*{Essay 3: Historical-Institutional Crisis Analysis}

\emph{Re-visiting the Political Economy of the Rise and Fall of the Unidad Popular: Towards a Global Political Economy Approach} asks how external finance, distributive conflict, international money, and central-bank solvency interacted during Chile's attempted socialist transformation. It places competing interpretations of the episode in dialogue with dependency theory, historical reconstruction, annual evidence, and monthly monetary and external data covering 1960--1980.

The historical account distinguishes five connected processes: the breakdown of Bretton Woods and global monetary instability; movements in world commodity prices and import costs; Chile-specific bilateral and commercial financial pressure; domestic distributive and political conflict; and the institutional response of the Central Bank of Chile (BCCh). Global monetary change altered the environment without mechanically causing the Chilean crisis. Targeted financial pressure narrowed the available policy space. Within that space, the BCCh chose to accommodate enterprise liquidity needs rather than impose immediate deflationary adjustment. Constraint did not eliminate agency.

The threshold estimates distinguish monetary transmission across solvency-growth states. In the adverse state, the reported response of base-money growth to inflation is more persistent and cumulatively larger than the inflation response to base-money growth. Forward monetary transmission remains significant, so accommodation does not mean that money has no effect on prices. These conditional responses support an interpretation of endogenous accommodation under external stress; they do not isolate an experimental counterfactual or establish an inevitable policy response. The essay's contribution is to explain why the historical crisis requires an account of international settlement and institutional choice beyond the long-run capacity relation.

\section*{Structure of the Dissertation}

The three essays follow the progression from measurement and identification, to structural mechanism and comparative extension, to historical-institutional crisis analysis. Each retains its own bibliography and appendices so that its argument and evidence can be evaluated on their own terms. A consolidated conclusion then draws together their implications and limits: capacity measurement depends on the relations governing capacity formation, while the reproduction of a peripheral economy also depends on external and monetary conditions that require their own historical explanation.
